1. Summary

Moscow decided in principle to leave Afghanistan in October 1985 — and then took more than three further years to actually go. This chapter argues that the delay was not drift.

3. Narrative

The new man reads the letters

Mikhail Gorbachev was elected General Secretary by the Politburo on 11 March 1985, after Chernenko's death. Within about two months he had told confidants and the Politburo that the troops should prepare to come home — that is Fred Halliday's reconstruction from declassified Soviet documents and interviews with former officials.

The language of justification began to move: where Brezhnev had spoken of internationalist duty to a fraternal party, Gorbachev's circle started talking about cost — letters from ordinary citizens, casualties, the drag on reform. The April 1985 Politburo discussions set the pattern for everything after: the war would continue for now, but its purpose was redefined as creating the conditions for leaving.

Afghan leaders were told, in effect, to widen their base and take the fighting onto their own shoulders while the 40th Army held the line. The US Army War College's Parameters history of the withdrawal (2011) quotes Gorbachev charging Karmal to "widen your social base," pursue dialogue with the tribes, court the clergy — and, in Halliday's rendering of the same encounter, to "give up all ideas of socialism."

Then came the decision in principle. At the Politburo session of 17 October 1985 Gorbachev read out citizens' letters expressing anger at the war, quoted the dissatisfaction in the country, and said the leadership must make a final decision on Afghanistan. His note-taker Chernyaev records him describing his meeting with Babrak Karmal in these words:

"We will help you, but with arms only, not troops."

— Gorbachev to Karmal, October 1985, as noted by Anatoly Chernyaev; published in the National Security Archive briefing book "Afghanistan and the Soviet Withdrawal 1989 — 20 Years Later"

"We will help you, but with arms only, not troops."

The Archive's editors stress what that sentence did and did not mean: the Soviet leaders had decided they would disengage, but they set no timetable until the autumn of 1987. A month later, on 17 November 1985, Gorbachev told the Politburo he had warned Karmal that Soviet forces would be out "by the summer of 1986" (Halliday). That date came and went. The threat was pressure, not a plan.

"A bleeding wound"

The public signal came at the 27th Congress of the CPSU, which met in Moscow from 25 February to 6 March 1986 — Gorbachev's first congress as leader, attended by nearly 5,000 delegates. Reporting the speech on 26 February 1986, The New York Times gave the two sentences that mattered:

Afghanistan, Gorbachev said, was a "bleeding wound," and the Soviet Union would like to withdraw its troops "in the nearest future."

— The New York Times, 26 February 1986, reporting the 27th Congress speech

Afghanistan, Gorbachev said, was a "bleeding wound," and the Soviet Union would like to withdraw its troops "in the nearest future."

Note the construction. The wound is the war's cost to the Soviet Union, not to Afghanistan. The withdrawal is a wish, not an order. Between those two sentences sits the whole method of the next three years: name the cost out loud, commit to nothing dated, and use the interval to build the exit.

Three options, two rejections

As Kalinovsky reconstructs in A Long Goodbye: The Soviet Withdrawal from Afghanistan (2011) — the standard scholarly account of the exit, and this chapter's spine — Moscow weighed three courses in 1985–86. First, fight to win with more force: another large troop commitment, sealed borders, a war carried into the Pakistani sanctuary. Second, hold the line while negotiating. Third, leave.

The first option died on the military's own testimony. Chief of the General Staff Akhromeyev told the political leadership there was no military solution at any sane price: victory would have required several hundred thousand more men and the sealing of a border the 40th Army had never been able to seal, plus a war with Pakistan that nobody wanted.

So the generals who had prosecuted the war told the politicians it could not be won — and the politicians chose the second option as the road to the third: hold, negotiate, and leave. That left the question the first two chapters posed in reverse.

The old question was why the army could not win. The new question was why, having admitted it, the army could not simply march home. The answer had three parts, and Moscow worked them in parallel: a client, a treaty, and a timetable.

The client: from Karmal to Najibullah

Karmal had to go because he would not play his assigned part. Gorbachev's charge — broaden the regime, talk to tribes and clergy, drop the socialism — assumed an Afghan leader willing to dilute the very party that was his power base. Karmal, in Moscow's reading, sat in Kabul waiting for Soviet troops to do the work.

In May 1986 Moscow acted: Karmal resigned as PDPA General Secretary on 4 May 1986 and Najibullah, the former KHAD security chief, replaced him; in November 1986 Karmal lost the chairmanship of the Revolutionary Council too — to Haji Mohammad Chamkani, deliberately not a PDPA member — and was exiled to Moscow.

Najibullah's line, launched in 1987, was called national reconciliation. On 15 January 1987 he offered the mujahideen a six-month ceasefire and a share in politics.

Late in 1987 a Loya Jirga ratified a new constitution: the word "Democratic" came out of the state's name, Islam returned as the state religion, a multi-party system appeared on paper, and an elected parliament with a prime minister replaced the Revolutionary Council as the face of rule. Government posts were opened to non-communists; exiled businessmen were invited to reclaim property; there was even talk of the king's return.

The mujahideen answer was no. At a gathering in Ghor province in July 1987, called by the Herat commander Ismail Khan, the resistance rejected the offer and the ceasefire lapsed. Nothing about the programme overcame the one fact that mattered: Najibullah was the Soviet clients' policeman, and no constitutional rewrite changed whose tanks kept him in Kabul.

So Moscow bought the client time instead of legitimacy. Frontier garrisons — Khost, Jalalabad, Kandahar, Herat — were stocked, fortified and left to hold as Soviet troops thinned. And the aid tap stayed open: weapons, fuel, wheat and cash.

The standard Western estimate, Steve Coll's in Ghost Wars (2004), puts the post-withdrawal flow at roughly $3 billion a year — an estimate, not an audited figure, and it should be read as one. What is documented is the mechanism: Soviet crews kept flying, Soviet missiles kept arriving, and Kabul's army was re-equipped as an army meant to fight without Soviet infantry beside it.

What changed on the battlefield: the Stinger, carefully

In September 1986 the first American FIM-92 Stinger shoulder-fired missiles reached mujahideen hands through Pakistan's ISI. The first engagement, near Jalalabad on 25 September 1986, downed three Mi-24 helicopter gunships in quick succession; CIA histories credit roughly 2,000–2,500 missiles supplied overall and claim about 269 Soviet aircraft downed. Soviet pilots changed their profiles within weeks.

Everything beyond it is disputed, and this chapter presents the dispute rather than picking a winner. Alan Kuperman, writing in Foreign Affairs ("Stinging Rebukes," from archival evidence), argues the missiles cannot have driven the decision: Gorbachev had resolved to seek the exit before the first Stinger was ever fired, and the early shock wore off within months as Soviet flares, beacons, exhaust baffles and changed tactics blunted the weapon.

Milton Bearden — the CIA station chief who ran the pipeline — and James Risen answer, in The Main Enemy (2004) and in Business Insider's retrospective interviews, that the kill count misses the point: the Stinger forced Soviet aviation above its effective envelope, which gave the mujahideen freedom of movement on the ground. Pakistani and Soviet sources, meanwhile, dispute the CIA's kill figures downward. Gorbachev himself later said the Stinger was not a major factor in the withdrawal decision.

Hold all three readings at once: a real tactical shock, largely countered within a year, arriving after the political decision it is often credited with causing. The mujahideen also shifted tactics in these years — heavier weapons, bolder attacks on fixed posts and convoys, attempts to take and hold border garrisons — and the attempts to take the border posts failed.

Magistral: the road to Khost

Operation Magistral ran from 19 November 1987 to 10 January 1988: Gromov's 40th Army, with Afghan government troops under Shahnawaz Tanai, forced the Gardez–Khost road through the Sata-Kandow pass, where Haqqani's men held dug-in blocking positions with rockets, heavy machine guns and anti-aircraft guns. The first convoys reached Khost at the end of December 1987.

The set-piece inside the operation — the 39-man 9th Company of the 345th Airborne holding Hill 3234 from 7 January 1988 against repeated assaults — became the late war's most mythologised engagement. Magistral worked, on its own terms: the siege was lifted and the garrison stocked.

It also proved the exact point Moscow did not want proved: the road stayed open only while Soviet battalions stood on it. When they left, the mujahideen cut it again — as they had done since 1981.

Why Geneva was the gate

The negotiation that made the exit possible had been running since June 1982: proximity talks in Geneva, brokered by the UN, nominally between Afghanistan and Pakistan, with the superpowers behind the curtain and Diego Cordovez shuttling between rooms. Twelve rounds across six years produced almost nothing while Moscow still hoped the war might be won.

Gorbachev's decision changed what the channel was for. From 1985 the US–Soviet bilateral track on regional issues ran alongside Geneva, and the deadlock broke only when Moscow accepted that the accords would not deliver a friendly Kabul — merely a covered exit. The last round ended on 8 April 1988, when Cordovez announced every instrument finalised.

On 14 April 1988 four instruments were signed at Geneva — the Agreements on the Settlement of the Situation Relating to Afghanistan:

  1. A bilateral Afghanistan–Pakistan agreement on non-interference and non-intervention;
  2. A US–Soviet declaration of international guarantees;
  3. A bilateral Afghanistan–Pakistan agreement on the voluntary return of refugees;
  4. An agreement on the interrelationships between the instruments — the clock: entry into force 15 May 1988, half the troops out by 15 August 1988, all out within nine months.

Monitoring fell to UNGOMAP, the UN Good Offices Mission. And here is the hole that mattered: the mujahideen were not parties, signed nothing, and rejected the accords outright. The non-interference instrument bound Kabul and Islamabad on paper — but the US–Pakistan pipeline to the resistance was never symmetrically cut.

Moscow had wanted a clause stopping American arms once the withdrawal finished; what it got, in place of that clause, was a contradictory bilateral "understanding" under which the supply continued. Gorbachev felt betrayed and went ahead anyway — which tells you what Geneva was: not a peace settlement, but a permission slip, priced in advance.

The timetable and its politics

The timetable was not set until the autumn of 1987 — two full years after the decision in principle. Inside the Politburo the argument was about duration: the military leadership wanted two years to get out safely; Gorbachev and Shevardnadze insisted on one.

The soldiers' case was professional: a staged extraction under fire, depots to empty, garrisons to stock, the Salang road to keep open. The politicians' case was political: a long goodbye would be renegotiated to death by its enemies, in Kabul and in the field. The politicians won. Gorbachev announced the decision publicly on 8 February 1988; the clock from Geneva gave nine months.

Phase one ran 15 May – 15 August 1988: roughly half the force left, on schedule, and UNGOMAP verified the columns. Then a three-month pause Moscow had announced in May — and then silence. Between 15 August 1988 and 1 January 1989 UNGOMAP observed no significant withdrawal.

Soviet representatives said the resumption was postponed "in the light of prevailing conditions" while reaffirming the final date. The conditions were real enough: mujahideen attacks on departing columns, a Kabul government terrified of being left alone, and a last argument inside Moscow — Shevardnadze and the KGB chief urging Gorbachev to leave a "volunteer" contingent guarding the road to Kabul. Gorbachev refused.

Phase two ran from mid-November 1988 to 15 February 1989. The fighting did not stop for the timetable. In January 1989 Soviet forces launched Operation Typhoon, beginning 23 January, against Massoud's positions astride the Kabul–Salang road — the last large Soviet operation of the war, fought to keep the northern exit open.

Convoys ground through the Salang tunnel under guard while the rearguard fought off the hills. On 15 February 1989, at the Hairatan–Termez Friendship Bridge, Lieutenant-General Boris Gromov walked over last behind his armoured column. Soviet television crews were waiting; Gromov swore at them. No member of the Politburo had come to greet the returning army — a detail Kalinovsky records, and the veterans did not forget it.

The people who did not want to go

Name them, because the resistance was institutional, not personal. The Defence Ministry's men in Kabul — above all Varennikov, head of the operational group — argued that the timetable abandoned the client: Najibullah's army was not ready, the border posts would fall, the withdrawal would read as defeat whatever Geneva called it. Elements of the General Staff warned that nine months was too fast for an extraction under fire.

Akhromeyev's position was the subtler one, and the one that matters: the chief of staff who had told the Politburo the war could not be won also told it the exit must be orderly — he backed leaving, and quarrelled only about how. Shevardnadze's answer, given repeatedly in 1987–88, was that every extra month raised the price of the same outcome: the client would not grow stronger by waiting, the Americans would not cut the pipeline by waiting, and the army would go on bleeding for a timetable rather than a purpose.

Gorbachev's answer was shorter: Kabul had panicked when it learned Moscow meant to leave — which proved, to him, that the dependency would never cure itself. You do not wean a client by feeding it longer.

The cost ledger as it stood in 1989

The Soviet official figures, published by the General Staff and repeated ever since: 14,453 dead — 9,511 killed in action, 2,386 dead of wounds, 2,556 dead of disease and accidents — with 53,753 wounded, 312 missing or captured (119 later freed), 451 aircraft lost including 333 helicopters, and 415,932 hospitalisations for disease, a number that says as much about the war's hygiene as its fighting.

Note the contest: the same General Staff's internal reporting, cited in later Russian publications, implies killed totals above 26,000 — so even the Soviet ledger disagrees with itself, and this chapter reports both numbers with their sources rather than reconciling them. The Afghan figures are worse and far less certain.

The Democratic Republic's own army lost upwards of 58,000 killed between 1980 and 1989; mujahideen dead are tentatively estimated at 75,000–90,000; civilian dead are estimated anywhere from around 560,000 to 2,000,000, with roughly a million the most commonly repeated figure and some five million refugees in Pakistan and Iran. Every one of these numbers is contested — counting the dead was nobody's priority while the dying went on — and the honest presentation is the range, not a point.

The money is the least documented column. Audited Soviet totals were never published; the working figure in Western scholarship is Coll's roughly $3 billion a year flowing to Kabul at the end, and it should be cited as Coll's estimate, not as a fact. What is documented is that the flow continued after the troops left — fuel, wheat, weapons, cash — and that it kept Najibullah's government alive for three more years.

The veterans came home to a country that did not know what to do with them. The afgantsy — hundreds of thousands of returning soldiers, many wounded, many carrying heroin habits picked up in Afghanistan — found no parades and thin aftercare. Braithwaite's Afgantsy (2011) documents the reception in detail: official silence, public ambivalence, and a veterans' movement that learned to organise outside the party's structures.

Coda, 1989–1990: the "Afghan syndrome" argument

Did the war break the Soviet Union's faith in its army and its party — and thereby help set the political weather of 1989–1990? The "Afghan syndrome" reading says yes: the war, fought on television and in citizens' letters, delegitimated the military, fed the glasnost press its first great scandal, and taught a generation that the party could be wrong about life and death.

It also has critics, and this chapter does not adjudicate between them — it reports the split. The critics' case: the Soviet collapse of 1989–90 was driven overwhelmingly by the economy, the nationalities crisis and the party's own disintegration, forces far larger than one lost war; Kalinovsky's account stresses that the withdrawal itself was managed and orderly, not a rout, which cuts against any simple "defeat destroyed the system" story.

What happened after 1990 — the end of the USSR, the end of Najibullah — is out of this course's scope. The story this course tells ends with an army walking north over a bridge, swearing at the cameras, into a country that had already decided not to thank it.

4. Machinery

This chapter establishes the mechanism of the face-saving exit: why a patron that has decided to leave still cannot leave, and what has to be built before it can. The mechanism has three interlocking parts, and each maps to a phase of 1985–89.

First, the client must be rebuilt as something that can be abandoned. A patron cannot leave a client that collapses the week after the last column crosses — not because the patron loves the client, but because instant collapse converts withdrawal into visible defeat. Hence the Karmal–Najibullah swap, the reconciliation programme, the constitution, the stocked garrisons: none of it needed to win. It needed to make Kabul survive long enough that the exit could be called a handover. (It worked, on exactly those terms: Najibullah outlived the Soviet Union's interest by three years.)

Second, the exit needs a treaty that renames it. Geneva did not end the war — the mujahideen never signed, the arms kept flowing, the fighting continued through the withdrawal. What Geneva did was give every signatory a story: Moscow withdrew "under the accords," Islamabad signed "non-interference" while the pipeline ran, Washington guaranteed while arming. The lesson generalises: settlements that enable exits are often not peace agreements at all. They are choreography — documents whose function is to let armed actors move without admitting defeat.

Third, the army must be extracted in stages it can survive. Nine months, two phases, a pause, the Salang convoys, Typhoon: the timetable was a military operation in its own right, fought against an enemy that understood exactly what a column on a mountain road is. The military's resistance to the one-year timetable was not (only) obstruction — it was a professional warning about what extraction under fire costs. The politicians overruled it because delay had its own cost: every month spent perfecting the exit was another month of bleeding for the timetable itself.

Carry this mechanism forward as a lens: whenever a great power announces it is leaving a small war, ask who the client is, what document renames the leaving, and what road the army takes home. In 1985–89 the answers were Najibullah, Geneva, and the Salang highway — and assembling all three is why four years passed between the decision and the bridge.
