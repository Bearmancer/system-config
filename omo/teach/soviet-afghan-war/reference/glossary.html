Glossary — as of chapter 3

Covers chapters 1–3. The lessons use these canonical spellings throughout; variants found in sources are flagged under the entry. Cast map · Timeline · Chapter index

People

Leonid Brezhnev. General Secretary of the CPSU Central Committee; the ailing ultimate authority who signs off the invasion 8–12 December 1979 and dies in November 1982. Taraki's murder weeks after the Moscow embrace is, on the memoir record, his personal turning point.

Yuri Andropov. KGB chairman, then General Secretary (November 1982 – February 1984). Driver of the intervention case from October 1979 and author of the early-December memo to Brezhnev; as leader hints to Zia that troops leave if Pakistan cuts the pipeline.

Dmitri Ustinov. Minister of Defence; co-driver with Andropov. Insists on a 75,000-man force over his generals' objections and signs the 24 December movement directive; dies December 1984.

Andrei Gromyko. Minister of Foreign Affairs; compliant third member of the deciding core after, in Kornienko's account, giving up on US–Soviet relations.

Alexei Kosygin. Chairman of the Council of Ministers; lead voice against sending troops in March 1979 ("we must not surrender Afghanistan" — but not with troops). Absent sick in December and never signs the resolution.

Konstantin Chernenko. General Secretary, February 1984 – March 1985. Handwrites the 12 December 1979 resolution so no typist sees it; his rule is the interregnum under which the war runs on autopilot.

Mikhail Gorbachev. General Secretary from 11 March 1985; orders the exit. Decides in principle October 1985, names the "bleeding wound" February 1986, swaps Karmal for Najibullah, and imposes the nine-month timetable over the military's two-year case.

Eduard Shevardnadze. Foreign minister from July 1985; pushes the Geneva track and the faster timetable, arguing every extra month raises the price of the same outcome.

Sergei Akhromeyev. Chief of the General Staff; tells the Politburo there is no military solution at any sane price, then quarrels only about how the exit is run, not whether.

Valentin Varennikov. Head of the Defence Ministry operational group in Kabul; argues the timetable abandons the client and reads as defeat whatever Geneva calls it.

Boris Gromov. Takes command of the 40th Army in 1987; runs Magistral and the withdrawal, and on 15 February 1989 walks over the Friendship Bridge last, swearing at the television crews.

Muhammad Daoud Khan. Prime minister in 1955 at the Soviet arms-and-credit turn; president 1973–78 after the 17 July 1973 coup against Zahir Shah. Spends five years shedding his communist kingmakers and is killed with most of his family in the Arg on 28 April 1978.

Nur Muhammad Taraki. General Secretary of the PDPA (Khalq) and head of state after the Saur revolution; launches the radical 1978 reforms, begs Moscow for disguised troops 18 March 1979, and is smothered 8 October 1979 on Amin's orders.

Hafizullah Amin. Taraki's deputy and organiser of the Saur coup, ruler from 14 September 1979. Purges Parcham, receives Archer Blood 27 October 1979, and is killed 27 December 1979 in Operation Storm-333.

Babrak Karmal. Leader of the Parcham faction; exiled as ambassador to Czechoslovakia July 1978, sheltered by Moscow, installed 28 December 1979. Removed as party leader 4 May 1986 and as head of state November 1986, then exiled to Moscow.

Mohammad Najibullah. Former KHAD security chief; party leader from May 1986, president from 1987. Author of the national-reconciliation line: ceasefire offer, new constitution, Islam restored, parties on paper. Survives three years on Soviet aid after the troops leave.

Ahmad Shah Massoud. Jamiat field commander in the Panjshir; survives nine offensives 1980–85, signs the January 1983 ceasefire with Tkachev and breaks it, and is struck by Operation Typhoon in January 1989.

Gulbuddin Hekmatyar. Leader of Hezb-e Islami; the ISI's favoured recipient with the strongest pipeline share; party rival of Massoud.

Burhanuddin Rabbani. Leader of Jamiat-e Islami; Massoud's party chief and the political face of the northern front.

Jalaluddin Haqqani. Field commander in the southeast out of Miranshah, originally with Yunus Khalis's Hezb faction; holds the Sata-Kandow blocking position against Magistral.

Ismail Khan. Captain of the mutinied 17th Division at Herat; later commander around Herat and host of the July 1987 Ghor gathering that rejects reconciliation. Whether he led the 1979 revolt itself is contested — supporters claim it, the detailed account denies it.

Abdul Haq. Commander in Kabul and the east, named among the war's principal field commanders.

Jimmy Carter. US President to January 1981. Signs the 3 July 1979 finding, amends it after the invasion, and answers with the Doctrine, the embargo, and the Olympic boycott.

Zbigniew Brzezinski. US National Security Adviser. Frames the invasion as expansionism toward the Indian Ocean (26 December 1979 memo) and later claims the July 1979 aid was meant to "induce" intervention — the "Afghan trap" claim, contested by Coll, Tobin and others.

Ronald Reagan. US President from January 1981. Turns Cyclone into the centrepiece doctrine, multiplies funding, approves the Stinger shipment, and co-guarantees Geneva.

Mohammad Zia-ul-Haq. Pakistan's ruler. Grants sanctuary, makes the ISI the sole arms conduit, favours Hekmatyar, and signs for Pakistan at Geneva while the pipeline runs on.

Diego Cordovez. UN Under-Secretary-General; runs the Geneva proximity channel from June 1982 to the April 1988 signing, shuttling between rooms.

Zahir Shah. King from 1933, deposed by Daoud in 1973; a background presence, with even talk of his return surfacing under reconciliation.

Nadir Shah. Takes the throne in 1929, between Amanullah and Zahir Shah; the arrangement with Moscow survives him.

Sayed Daoud Tarun. Amin's police chief, killed in the 14 September 1979 palace shoot-out while Amin escapes wounded.

Mir Akbar Khyber. Parchami whose murder on 17 April 1978 triggers Daoud's arrest order and hence the Saur coup.

Adolph Dubs. US ambassador sent to engage the Saur regime; kidnapped and killed in a botched rescue, February 1979.

Archer Blood. Acting American chargé received alone by Amin for forty minutes on 27 October 1979; his routine cable (Kabul 7726) reads in Moscow as a flip in progress.

Anatoly Tkachev (Tkachyov). GRU colonel — lieutenant-colonel in some renderings; the chapters use Tkachev — who negotiates the January 1983 ceasefire with Massoud directly.

Shahnawaz Tanai. Afghan government commander alongside Gromov in Operation Magistral.

Haji Mohammad Chamkani. Deliberately not a PDPA member; receives the Revolutionary Council chairmanship from Karmal in November 1986.

Yunus Khalis. Head of the Hezb faction Haqqani originally fights under; named once, a reminder the "parties" are themselves splinters.

Yuri Tukharinov. Lieutenant General commanding the freshly re-created 40th Army as it crosses on 25 December 1979.

Parties and factions

PDPA (People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan). Founded 1 January 1965 with Taraki as general secretary; seizes power in the Saur revolution and never escapes the split described below.

Khalq ("Masses"). Taraki's faction with Amin as organiser: Pashtun rural cadres and army officers, radical and fast. The dispute the chapters flag: Khalq vs Parcham is not a footnote quarrel but the fracture that governs — Khalqis purge Parchamites 1978–79 (Pul-e-Charkhi, thousands dead), then murder each other (Taraki, October 1979), leaving Karmal to rule over the faction that shot his own.

Parcham ("Banner"). Karmal's faction: urban, Dari-speaking, gradualist. Purged and exiled in 1978, it returns on Soviet bayonets in December 1979 to govern ministries and an army still full of Khalqis.

Hezb-e Islami. Hekmatyar's party, headquartered in Peshawar; disciplined, Islamist, and — decisively — closest to Zia, so the ISI's favoured recipient with the strongest pipeline share. (Yunus Khalis leads a separate Hezb faction under which Haqqani originally serves.)

Jamiat-e Islami. Rabbani's party; its strength lies in field commanders, above all Massoud, rather than headquarters. Party rival of Hezb-e Islami as well as enemy of Kabul.

Seven-party alliance (Islamic Unity of Afghanistan Mujahideen). The seven Sunni Peshawar parties' formal front, announced 16 May 1985 under US–Saudi–Pakistani pressure with a joint military committee and communiqué. The dispute: Prashad argues a 1981 founding with Saudi financing, Lansford dates the formal coalition to 1985; either way it is a united front for world opinion, not a unified command, and changes little on the ground.

Shia parties. Backed from Iran and standing entirely outside the Peshawar system and its ISI-controlled funding.

Places and geography

Kabul. The capital the contingent is ordered to hold; seat of every regime in the course and of the regimes' prisons.

Arg palace. Daoud's seat and death place, 28 April 1978.

Tajbeg Palace. Amin's palace above Kabul, stormed the evening of 27 December 1979 in Storm-333.

Panjshir Valley. Massoud's gorge north of Kabul; nine Soviet offensives 1980–85 take it, declare him destroyed, withdraw, and watch him reconstitute. The January 1983 ceasefire leaves only the Anava garrison, whose access the mujahideen control.

Salang highway and tunnel. The Soviet-built road through the Hindu Kush; with the ring road, the axle of the whole war — every operation road-bound, every withdrawal convoy tunnel-bound.

Ring road. The Kabul–Kandahar–Herat–Mazar circuit the plan orders seized first and garrisoned throughout.

Herat. Western city of the March 1979 uprising and 17th Division mutiny, the deadliest episode of the pre-war period; later Ismail Khan's ground.

Kandahar. Southern garrison city on the Kushka axis; one of the stocked frontier garrisons of the exit years.

Khost, Gardez, and the Sata-Kandow pass. Eastern garrison (Khost), its supply town (Gardez), and the dug-in pass between them where Haqqani blocks the road until Magistral forces it.

Jalalabad. Eastern city near which the first Stingers are fired, 25 September 1986; another stocked frontier garrison.

Termez–Hairatan and the Friendship Bridge. The northern crossing: divisions roll down it in December 1979 and Gromov walks back over it last on 15 February 1989.

Kushka–Herat–Shindand axis. The western invasion route toward Kandahar.

Bagram. Airbase where the 103rd Guards Airborne Division lands 25 December 1979.

Pul-e-Khumri. Northern road town on the Termez–Kabul axis.

Pul-e-Charkhi prison. Kabul prison where the Khalq regime kills between 10,000 and 27,000 (Roy's wider estimate: 50,000–100,000 disappeared in the Taraki–Amin period).

Durand Line. The border Afghanistan refuses to recognise; the issue that pushes Daoud toward Moscow in 1955 and keeps Kabul and Islamabad hostile throughout.

Pashtunistan. The claimed Pashtun homeland inside Pakistan; Kabul's pressing of the claim reads as encirclement in Islamabad and as a nuisance in Moscow.

Peshawar (and Miranshah). The Pakistani cities where the Sunni parties headquarter themselves — recruits, rest areas, hospitals, training camps and arms bazaars across a border the 40th Army may not cross in strength.

Ghor province. Site of the July 1987 gathering, called by Ismail Khan, that rejects Najibullah's reconciliation.

Hill 3234. The set-piece inside Magistral: 39 men of the 9th Company, 345th Airborne, holding from 7 January 1988 against repeated assaults.

Operations and institutions

Operation Storm-333. The 27 December 1979 KGB special-units assault on the Tajbeg Palace; Amin — reportedly told that evening "the Soviets will help us," then that it is the Soviets attacking — killed with family members, bodies wrapped in carpets for unmarked graves. The RFE/RL fortieth-anniversary account is the chapters' fullest source.

Operation Cyclone. The CIA programme: $695,000 of cash, non-military supplies and propaganda under the 3 July 1979 finding ("either directly or through third countries"), amended January 1980 to money then weapons through the ISI, matched dollar-for-dollar by Riyadh, rising from $20–30 million a year under Carter to the centrepiece doctrine under Reagan. The dispute it carries: whether Washington meant to provoke the invasion — Brzezinski's 1998 "Afghan trap" claim versus Coll, Harrison, Riedel, Cogan and the January-1980 arms record.

Limited contingent (ogranichenny kontingent). The official name of the Soviet force — and the course's central trap: orders to hold cities, roads, airfields and infrastructure while the Afghan army fights, on the assumption of a months-long mission. The dispute is retrospective, not textual: nobody contests the name, only whether anyone in December understood what "limited" would cost.

40th Army. Re-created days before Christmas 1979 in the Turkestan Military District; ~30,000 initial combat strength around the 108th and 5th Guards Motor Rifle Divisions plus airborne troops; never much above ~100,000 men, tied to two highways.

Afghan Army (DRA forces). From ~100,000–110,000 (1978) to ~25,000–30,000 (1980) through desertion, mutiny and purges — "melting away like an ice floe in a tropical sea" (US embassy cable). Karmal's hope of disbanding it is vetoed by Soviet advisers; divisions average a quarter of establishment by mid-decade.

KGB. Andropov's service: authors the intervention case, shelters Karmal, storms the palace. Its Afghan counterpart under Najibullah is the KHAD security police he once headed.

ISI. Pakistan's Inter-Services Intelligence under Zia: sole distributor of the pipeline, active manager of the sanctuary, and selector of winners — the Islamist parties closest to Zia, above all Hekmatyar, receive the lion's share.

CIA. Funder of Cyclone through the ISI; station chief Milton Bearden runs the pipeline and later defends the Stinger's tactical effect against Kuperman.

MI6. Britain's service, running its own channel into the pipeline alongside the Egyptian and Chinese Soviet-pattern weapons flows.

Politburo. The full body that refuses troops 17–18 March 1979 and the shrunken core (Andropov, Ustinov, Gromyko, Brezhnev) that assents 8–12 December — the course's "rooms that cannot dissent."

Geneva Accords (Agreements on the Settlement of the Situation Relating to Afghanistan). Four instruments signed 14 April 1988, in force 15 May: non-interference, US–Soviet guarantees, refugee return, and the clock (half out by 15 August, all out within nine months). What they did not cover is the point: the mujahideen never sign, reject them outright, and the pipeline is never symmetrically cut — Moscow wanted an arms-cutoff clause and got a contradicted "understanding" instead. A permission slip, priced in advance; not a peace settlement.

Geneva proximity talks. The channel itself: Pakistan and Afghanistan in the same building from June 1982, never the same room, Cordovez shuttling; "95 percent" complete by April 1983 and stalled on the post-withdrawal government until Gorbachev repurposes it as the exit's choreography.

UNGOMAP. The UN Good Offices Mission that verifies the columns: half out on schedule, then "no significant withdrawal" 15 August 1988 – 1 January 1989, then phase two to the bridge.

Loya Jirga. The traditional assembly Najibullah convenes late in 1987 to ratify the constitution (exact sitting date contested in the sources, so the lesson says "late 1987").

Carter Doctrine. The January 1980 State of the Union declaration that the Persian Gulf is a vital American interest to be defended by force — with embargo, SALT II shelved, and the boycott alongside.

Reagan Doctrine. Support to anti-communist insurgencies as doctrine; the mujahideen its centrepiece and Cyclone its best-funded case.

SALT II. The arms-limitation treaty signed June 1979; never reaches a Senate vote after the invasion.

SEATO and CENTO (Baghdad Pact). The Western alliances Pakistan joins in 1954–55; the encirclement that sends Daoud to Moscow's wallet.

National reconciliation. Najibullah's 1987 line: 15 January six-month ceasefire offer, opened posts, reclaimed property, a constitution with Islam and parties on paper — rejected at Ghor in July because no rewrite changes whose tanks keep him in Kabul.

Operation Magistral. 19 November 1987 – 10 January 1988: Gromov with Tanai forces the Gardez–Khost road through Sata-Kandow; the siege lifts, the garrison stocks — and proves the road stays open only while Soviet battalions stand on it.

Operation Typhoon. From 23 January 1989, against Massoud astride the Kabul–Salang road: the last large Soviet operation, fought to keep the northern exit open.

Panjshir offensives (I–VII). Nine operations 1980–85, culminating in the 12,000-man spring 1982 assault and the 15,000-man Panjshir VII of April–September 1984: seal, bomb, declare, withdraw, watch him return.

Terms

bi-tarafi. "Without sides": Kabul's standing doctrine of playing the great powers against each other while joining no bloc.

Saur. The second month of the Afghan calendar, April–May; the 27–28 April 1978 coup is named for it.

mujahideen / jihad. "Those waging jihad": the course uses the lessons' spelling mujahideen throughout (not mujahedin/mujahedeen). The clergy gives the jihad its language and the tribes its manpower; neither gives it a single command.

Cargo 200. The sealed zinc coffins in which the dead come home; the casualties a state secret, the funerals public.

afgantsy. The returning veterans — hundreds of thousands, many wounded, many carrying heroin habits — met with official silence, public ambivalence, and thin aftercare.

Internationalist duty. The official language of the intervention under Brezhnev: assistance to a fraternal party. It erodes into bitter irony and is displaced, under Gorbachev, by the language of cost.

Bleeding wound. Gorbachev's 26 February 1986 construction at the 27th Congress: the wound is the war's cost to the Soviet Union, and the withdrawal a wish, not an order.

Afghan syndrome. The 1989–90 reading that the war delegitimated army and party and set the political weather of the collapse. Contested: holders in Russian public debate, Western press and some scholarship; critics weight economy, nationalities and party disintegration higher, with Kalinovsky stressing the withdrawal was managed, not a rout. The course leaves it contested.

Stinger (FIM-92). The American shoulder-fired missile, first fired 25 September 1986 near Jalalabad (three Mi-24s). The Stinger dispute: a real tactical shock — aviation forced above its effective envelope — largely countered within a year by flares, baffles and changed profiles, arriving after the October 1985 decision in principle it is often credited with causing. Kuperman presses the timing, Bearden and Risen the tactical effect, Gorbachev himself the dismissal; the chapters hold all three.

Article 4 / Article 51. The published legal pretext: Article 4 of the 1978 treaty ("appropriate measures" for security) plus staged Afghan requests plus Article 51 of the UN Charter. Historians treat the requests as manufactured — the requesting government was installed by the troops themselves.

DRA. Democratic Republic of Afghanistan; the 1987 constitution drops "Democratic," restores Islam as the state religion, and puts a multi-party parliament on paper.

KHAD. The Afghan security service Najibullah heads before his elevation; the policeman's past that sinks the reconciler's programme.
