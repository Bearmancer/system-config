Cast & relationship map — as of chapter 3

Covers chapters 1–3 (1919 – 15 February 1989). Names below are the course's canonical spellings; the lessons use these forms throughout. Course home · Chapter 1 · Chapter 2 · Chapter 3

How to read this map

Colour is the only line difference. Every edge is solid, the same weight (2.2 px), with a colour-matched arrowhead. There are no dashed, dotted or thicker lines: the colour tells you the kind of tie, and the label on the line tells you the detail. Person nodes are white; organisation nodes are cream (#f2efe4). Each node carries its chapter of introduction as (chN).

Roster

Moscow

  • Leonid Brezhnev (ch1). General Secretary; the ailing ultimate authority persuaded by the Andropov–Ustinov small group on 8 December 1979, who dies in November 1982. Supplies arms, aid and advisers to Taraki's Afghanistan.
  • Yuri Andropov (ch1). KGB chairman, then General Secretary (November 1982 – February 1984). Driver of the intervention case from October 1979; persuades Brezhnev with Ustinov in the small-group decision; shelters Karmal through covert channels; succeeded by Chernenko in February 1984.
  • Dmitri Ustinov (ch1). Defence Minister; co-driver with Andropov, demands the 75,000-man force, signs the 24 December movement directive, and strikes Amin in Operation Storm-333.
  • Andrei Gromyko (ch1). Foreign Minister; compliant third member of the deciding core; extends the December 1978 treaty tie to Taraki's Afghanistan.
  • Alexei Kosygin (ch1). Premier; lead voice against sending troops in the March 1979 debate with Ustinov, absent sick in December. His refusal is the road not taken.
  • Konstantin Chernenko (ch2). General Secretary, February 1984 – March 1985; succeeds Andropov and is succeeded by Gorbachev. The interregnum under which the war runs on autopilot.
  • Mikhail Gorbachev (ch2). General Secretary from March 1985; orders the exit. Removes Karmal in May 1986, arms the Najibullah client that replaces him, and overrules Varennikov's objections to the withdrawal timetable.
  • Eduard Shevardnadze (ch3). Foreign minister from July 1985; negotiates the Geneva settlement with Cordovez and pushes the faster one-year timetable.
  • Sergei Akhromeyev (ch3). Chief of the General Staff; tells the Politburo there is no military solution, then runs the exit through Gromov.
  • Valentin Varennikov (ch3). Head of the Defence Ministry operational group in Kabul; argues against Gorbachev's withdrawal timetable as an abandonment of the client.
  • Boris Gromov (ch3). Takes command of the 40th Army in 1987; forces the Khost road against Haqqani, strikes Massoud in Operation Typhoon, and walks over the Friendship Bridge last.
  • Politburo (ch2, organisation). Moscow command: installs Karmal and orders the 40th Army south.

Kabul

  • Muhammad Daoud Khan (ch1). President 1973–78 after the 17 July 1973 coup; drifts from Moscow and is overthrown and killed in the Saur revolution of April 1978 that brings Taraki to power.
  • Nur Muhammad Taraki (ch1). PDPA General Secretary (Khalq) and head of state after the Saur revolution; receives Soviet arms, aid and the December 1978 treaty; feuds with Amin and is murdered on 8 October 1979.
  • Hafizullah Amin (ch1). Taraki's deputy, then ruler from 14 September 1979; split from Taraki in the Khalq feud and from Karmal across the Khalq–Parcham divide; courts the US–Amin contact, and is killed in Storm-333.
  • Babrak Karmal (ch1). Parcham leader, sheltered by Moscow, installed 28 December 1979; commands the Afghan Army, is removed by Gorbachev in May 1986 and exiled.
  • Mohammad Najibullah (ch3). Party leader from May 1986, president from 1987; author of the national-reconciliation line; armed by Gorbachev as the survivable client and covered by the Geneva guarantee; besieged by proxy through Zia's pipeline.

Field commanders

  • Ahmad Shah Massoud (ch2). Jamiat commander in the Panjshir; target of the 40th Army's offensives and of Operation Typhoon; party rival of Hekmatyar.
  • Gulbuddin Hekmatyar (ch2). Leader of Hezb-e Islami; the ISI's favoured recipient through the arms conduit; party rival of Massoud and of Rabbani across the Jamiat–Hezb divide.
  • Burhanuddin Rabbani (ch2). Leader of Jamiat-e Islami; Massoud's party chief, locked in the Jamiat–Hezb rivalry with Hekmatyar.
  • Jalaluddin Haqqani (ch3). Field commander around Khost; holds the Sata-Kandow blocking position against Gromov's Khost road operation.
  • Ismail Khan (ch1). Officer of the mutinied 17th Division, later commander around Herat; fights the 40th Army's Herat operations after turning his guns on the regime in March 1979.
  • Abdul Haq (ch2). Commander in Kabul and the east; fights the 40th Army on the Kabul and east front.

Outside powers and mediator

  • Jimmy Carter (ch1). US President to January 1981; signs the 3 July 1979 finding through Brzezinski. Doctrine, embargo, boycott, and the Cyclone expansion follow.
  • Zbigniew Brzezinski (ch1). US National Security Adviser; frames the invasion as expansionism, carries Carter's 3 July finding, and opens the secret US–Amin contact.
  • Ronald Reagan (ch2). US President from January 1981; approves the Stinger shipment to Zia, underwrites the pipeline, and co-guarantees Geneva through Cordovez.
  • Mohammad Zia-ul-Haq (ch2). Pakistan's ruler; controls the pipeline tap, receives the Stingers, signs for Pakistan at Geneva, and keeps Najibullah under siege by proxy.
  • Diego Cordovez (ch3). UN mediator running the Geneva proximity channel; negotiates the settlement with Shevardnadze, delivers the guarantee to Najibullah and receives the US guarantee and Pakistan's signature.

Organisations

  • 40th Army (ch2). The limited contingent: ordered in by the Politburo, arms and advises the Afghan Army, and fights Massoud up the Panjshir, Ismail Khan around Herat, and Abdul Haq on the Kabul and east front.
  • Afghan Army (ch2). DRA forces: commanded by Karmal, armed and advised by the 40th Army, melting away throughout.
  • ISI (ch2). Pakistan's conduit: receives Cyclone funds from the CIA and arms Hekmatyar above all others.
  • CIA (ch2). Cyclone funder: feeds the pipeline through the ISI.

Context that is deliberately not drawn

Offstage actors — named in the chapters but given no node because no chapter draws them into a diagrammable tie: Zahir Shah (the exiled king, a background presence and a late rumour of return); Sayed Daoud Tarun (killed 14 September 1979, a death inside the feud, not a tie); Mir Akbar Khyber (whose murder triggers the Saur arrests); Adolph Dubs and Archer Blood as individuals (the Dubs killing and the Blood cable matter as events; only the US–Amin contact itself is drawn); Lieutenant General Tukharinov, Colonel Tkachev, Shahnawaz Tanai and Haji Mohammad Chamkani (operational and constitutional names, each confined to one episode); Yunus Khalis (named once as Haqqani's original party chief); and the collective patrons — Saudi Arabia, Egypt, China, Britain's MI6, Iran and the Shia parties — handled as the pipeline and the sanctuary in chapter 2 rather than as individual nodes. The mujahideen seven-party alliance of May 1985 is likewise an event on the timeline, not a node: it changed little on the ground.

Why not: a node on this map promises a tie, and inventing ties the chapters never show would mislead. Anything named here is defined instead in the glossary.
