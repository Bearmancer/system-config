Soviet-Afghan War: How Moscow Got In, And Why It Took Ten Years

Three chapters · built from the documentary record, with citations · chapter pages open with a cast block and a chapter-scoped relationship diagram

The question this course answers. The USSR and Afghanistan had decades of quiet, ordinary relations — aid agreements, trade, training, a border that was never a war front. Then in December 1979 Moscow sent an army across that border, and did not leave for ten years. The puzzle is the seam in the middle: how an invasion that looked sudden from outside was not sudden inside the machinery that ordered it, and why the leaving took years after the decision to leave was taken.

Chapter 1 answers the "suddenly". Chapters 2 and 3 answer the ten years — first why the war could not be won, then why it could not be ended.

How to read this course. There is no single source behind it, so there is no creator whose claims are being checked. The discipline instead is that every load-bearing date, figure and quotation carries its provenance, and that documented fact stays visibly apart from contested reading. Each chapter states that split in its own fact-check box.

The transcript-style apparatus of the other workspaces does not exist here (there was no source to correct), so the correction log in NOTES.md is empty by design, not by omission.

Chapters

  • 1919–1979
    Chapter 1 — The neighbour
    How the Soviet-Afghan connection was actually built over sixty years: the 1921 treaty, the arms and roads, the PDPA, the 1973 and 1978 coups, the Herat mutiny and the killing of Taraki — and then, inside the December 1979 machinery, the four meetings that turned a refusal to intervene into an airborne assault on Kabul. This is the chapter that dissolves the word "suddenly".
  • Dec 1979–1985
    Chapter 2 — The ten years, part one: why Moscow could not win
    What the 40th Army was actually ordered to do, and why the orders could not be carried out: a government that could not hold the countryside, a resistance with a sanctuary it could not be denied, terrain and doctrine that cancelled Soviet advantages, and a supply pipeline running from Washington, Riyadh and Islamabad into the mountains.
  • 1985–1989
    Chapter 3 — The ten years, part two: why leaving took four more years
    From the "bleeding wound" to the Friendship Bridge: how the new leadership decided in principle to leave, and then spent four years arranging a face-saving exit — a client to keep alive, a negotiation at Geneva, and an army that had to be pulled out under fire in stages. Plus the coda: what the withdrawal left behind in Moscow.
