Stalin: The Court of the Red Tsar — chapter 14

The Dwarf Rises; Casanova Falls

Chapter 14 of 58 · late January–1 May 1935

Lesson 01 · cast map · glossary

One chapter, two careers moving in opposite directions: a tiny, universally liked party secretary named Nikolai Yezhov is handed the Kirov case, a secretary's chair and the oversight of the NKVD and starts to look like the coming man; an old friend of Stalin's, the womanizing "Uncle Abel" Yenukidze, is put through a security case and expelled from the Party. In between: a death that may not be one, two promotions that reshuffle the elite, and a court learning to settle its own scores.

2. What matters — and what you can skip

Carry these three out of the chapter: (1) Yezhov's rise is the chapter's engine — Kirov case, Kirov's CC chair, NKVD oversight on 31 March — plus the colleague's verdict on him, "He does not know how to stop." (2) Yenukidze's fall runs on two rails: the catalyst is Stalin's fury over rewritten personal history, and the weapon is the Kremlin Case crowned by the Plenum demolition. (3) The 1 February reshuffle — Kaganovich to the railways, Moscow to Khrushchev — sets the personnel board the rest of Part Three plays on.

Skim or skip: the salon guest lists (Babel, Utsesov, Pilniak — colour, not mechanism); Yezhov's biography beyond the rise-and-flaw spine (Tartar Republic, Kirgizia, Personnel Department — background, not plot); the dacha footnotes (Lipki, Sukhumi) on a first pass. The mechanism in section 9 compresses all three essentials onto one screen.

3. A suspicious death opens the chapter (25 January 1935)

Valerian Kuibyshev, 47, dies "unexpectedly of heart disease and alcoholism", eight weeks after Kirov.

He had questioned the NKVD's handling of the Kirov investigation and sided with Kirov and Sergo — which is why claims appear that his doctors killed him, and why his name turns up on a list of those allegedly poisoned by Yagoda.

The book resists the neat version: he had been ill and drank heroically, his son Vladimir believed murder, and Montefiore's verdict is that some deaths in the 1930s were natural — while conceding that from here on every prominent death looks suspect.

4. Two promotions (1 February 1935)

Stalin redistributes power. Kaganovich takes the railways — the colossal job of moving a country — and hands Moscow to his protégé Khrushchev, who becomes the capital's First Secretary: peasant-miner's son, born 1894, met Kaganovich in Yuzovka during the February 1917 Revolution, once flirted with Trotskyism, and now has patrons in Kaganovich, Nadya and Stalin.

Coarse, cunning, tireless, he drives "Stalinist Moscow" — construction, churches demolished, the Metro — becomes a Kuntsevo regular, and regards himself as Stalin's "son". The book's phrase for him: a "pitiless, ambitious believer".

Footnote to the chapter: Mikoyan and Chubar move up to full Politburo members; Zhdanov and Eikhe take their candidate seats.

5. The Dwarf rises: Yezhov

Kaganovich's other protégé emerges as the coming man. Yezhov already runs the Kirov case; now he takes Kirov's place as CC Secretary, and on 31 March is formally designated to supervise the NKVD. The portrait is built out of contradictions:

  • Universally liked — "responsive, humane, gentle, tactful"; women in particular liked him. The face is almost "beautiful", the grin wide, the eyes bright green-blue, the hair thick and black.
  • An energetic workaholic in a crumpled cheap suit and blue satin shirt, chattering in his Leningrad accent; fine baritone, guitarist, gopak dancer — and, in a government of small men, 151 cm tall. He is the "dwarf" of the chapter title.
  • Autodidact from nowhere: born 1895 in a small Lithuanian town, father a forest warden who ran a tearoom-cum-brothel, mother a maid; a few years of primary school, then the Putilov Works; too short for the Tsarist army, he mended guns, joined the Red Army in 1919 and met his patron Kaganovich at Vitebsk. Then the ladder: the Tartar Republic (1921), secretary of Kirgizia (1925), the Communist Academy, the CC, Deputy Agriculture Commissar, an office meeting with Stalin in November 1930, Politburo attendance, the CC Personnel Assignments Department, and a role purging the Party in 1933.
  • The flaw, in a colleague's words: "He does not know how to stop." The chapter also shows the private underside — puerile humour, orgies, bisexuality (a lover, Goloshchekin, had organized the Romanov murder), model yachts as his only hobby — and a wrecked constitution: TB, angina, sciatica, psoriasis, "neurasthenia", depression, drink.
  • Stalin's care: rest cures, the nickname "my blackberry" (yezhevika), curt notes ("Give him some work"; "When you say something, you always do it!"), and a 1934 collapse in boils that sends him to the best medical care in Central Europe, with embassy dispatches on his health.

Nobody objects to the rise: Khrushchev admires the appointment; Bukharin sees a "good heart and clean conscience", though he notices the grovelling before Stalin. Vera Trail, whose memoir of meeting him remains unpublished, notices he could "finish one's sentences". The chapter's summary of him: sly, able, perceptive — and with no moral restraint. With Yagoda, he now works to force Zinoviev, Kamenev and their allies to confess that they were responsible for Kirov's murder and "all manner of other dastardly deeds".

6. Yezhov's marriage — and the poet in the attic

Mandelstam, the poet, happens to be staying at the same Sukhumi sanatorium in 1930 as Yezhov and his then wife, and witnesses the courtship: Antonina "Tonya" Titova — married 1919, educated, a sincere Marxist — sunbathing over Das Kapital while her husband rises early to cut roses for another (married) woman. Yezhov divorces her that year.

The new wife is Yevgenia Feigenberg: 26, from Gomel, slim, flashing eyes, a literary groupie with husbands behind her (Khayutin, then Gadun of the London embassy), who picked up Isaac Babel in Berlin — "You don't know me but I know you well" — and now keeps a salon: Babel, the jazz star Utsesov, Pilniak. The only magnate couple close to both Yezhovs is Sergo and Zina; their daughter Eteri remembers that Yevgenia was "much better dressed than the other Bolshevik wives".

7. Casanova falls: Yenukidze

The other half of the title. Abel Yenukidze — Nadya's godfather, an old friend Stalin had once begged to holiday with — has been living as the court's great sybarite: affairs with ever-younger girls, teenage ballerinas, down to children of nine to eleven, mothers paid off, his office a virtual dating agency. Maria Svanidze denounces the "stench" to Stalin, who had not trusted him since 1929.

The chapter identifies the catalyst as Stalin's favourite subject — personal history, which "for the Bolsheviks was what genealogy was for the medieval knights".

Yenukidze's book The Secret Bolshevik Printing Presses, forwarded by Pravda's Mekhlis with passages marked, draws Stalin's marginalia: "That's false!", "fibs", "balderdash!"; his article on Baku circulates with "Ha-ha-ha!". His grievous mistake was not lying enough about Stalin's heroic exploits — "What more does he want?" he complains. "He wants me to admit he is a genius." (Against him, the sycophants: Lakoba's 1934 history of Stalin in Batumi, and Beria's own falsified Transcaucasus book inscribed "To my dear, adored master, to the Great Stalin!")

Then the Kremlin Case: a "terrorist cell" is uncovered inside the Kremlin, 110 of Yenukidze's employees are arrested for terrorism, a legendary "Countess" is said to have poisoned book pages to kill Stalin, two people are sentenced to death and the rest to the camps. The chapter shows the case has several angles at once: Yenukidze, the clearing of the Kremlin, and Nadya — a maid is arrested for gossiping about her suicide, and Stalin remembered that Yenukidze had "swayed" Nadya politically and been first to see the body.

At the Plenum the attack is delivered by Yezhov and Beria: Zinoviev and Kamenev were not merely morally responsible for Kirov's murder, they planned it; "Uncle Abel" sheltered the terrorists, and "this nearly cost Comrade Stalin his life".

The exchanges come fast — Yenukidze to Yagoda ("No one was hired for work without security clearance!"), Beria on his loans to fallen comrades, Sergo ("So what if she starves to death…"), Voroshilov ("What are you? Some kind of child?"), Mikoyan ("You acted wrongly"), Stalin himself ("Deported by the Soviet authorities"), and Yagoda's self-criticism ("I did not seize Yenukidze by the throat"). Kaganovich supplies the line for the record: "Comrade Stalin at once smelled a rat." Expelled from the Central Committee and the Party, Yenukidze is sacked, made to publish a "Correction of Errors", and demoted to running a Caucasian sanatorium.

8. The private aftermath

At Kuntsevo, a grumpy Stalin suddenly smiles at Maria Svanidze: "Are you pleased Abel's been punished?" Later, on May Day, kebabs with Zhenya and the Svanidzes; Stalin is tense until the women start bickering, then the table toasts Nadya — "she crippled me. After condemning Yasha for shooting himself, how could Nadya kill herself?" The footnote quietly shows the household still rearranging itself around her memory: Svetlana asks for the dacha at Lipki that Nadya had chosen and decorated, and Stalin agrees even though "it was hard for Joseph to be there".

9. The mechanism this chapter teaches

  • Suspect death — the court's new normal; the book flags it and refuses to over-conclude.
  • Personal history as capital — a leader's biography is contested property; not praising it enough is a political act.
  • Security cases as household warfare — the Kremlin Case is aimed at a person, a building, and a memory at once.
  • Rise and fall are one machine — the man who now runs the Kirov case is the man who demolishes Uncle Abel.
