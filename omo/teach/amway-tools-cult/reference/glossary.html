The company & the two businesses

  • Amway / Amway Corporation — the soap-and-products company, founded 1959 (the video's chapter 2 says "1949"; that date belongs to Ja-Ri — see lesson 01). In the entry stories it looks minor: "just the corporation that manufactures the soap".
  • Ja-Ri Corporation — what DeVos and Van Andel actually formed in 1949; their Nutrilite distributorship vehicle; merged into Amway Corporation in 1964.
  • The American Way Association — incorporated April 1959; ancestor of the name "Amway" (short for "American Way").
  • Nutrilite — the organization DeVos and Van Andel sold for before Amway; the video calls it "generally regarded as the first multi-level marketing company" (checked in lesson 01: the multi-level system dates to 1945, Mytinger & Casselberry's national distributorship).
  • Richard DeVos & Jay Van Andel — Amway's co-founders; conservative Calvinists of the Christian Reformed Church. Chapter 4 adds a footnote: DeVos reportedly sponsored Fred Hanson, "one of the original Amway distributors", in the '50s (the record says the Basses — see lesson 02). Chapter 7 gives them the political move of the story: the private 1975 White House visit to Gerald Ford.
  • The two pyramids (tools vs Amway) — the video's central structural claim (ch3): the Amway MLM business and the tools business are two overlapping pyramids — separate ownership, separate leadership, historically acrimonious, symbiotic, and unable to exist without each other. The larger, semi-hidden one is the tools cult: "where most of the money is and a lot of the abuses occur". Amway's FTC-blessed legality covers only the smaller pyramid; the tools business's legality is, in the video's phrase, untested.
  • Tools / BSMs (business support materials) — the motivational products sold to distributors: tapes, books, live seminars and rallies; later a voicemail system called Amvox. Circa 1980: ~$5 a tape (~$17 today), about 60¢ to make; sold through standing orders and at rally tables (up to $10,000 cash a night). The video's whole subject — "the larger pyramid".
  • Standing order — the arrangement by which a distributor agrees to buy a tape (usually weekly) regardless of what it is, until cancelled — "which it would almost never be".
  • LOC / Liquid Organic Cleaner — Amway's first product (1959), originally "Frisk"; with SA8 laundry detergent among the company's first products. On the Miami tape a kingpin concedes the point both ways: "SA8 is a great product… LOC is a great product. But it's not going to stand alone" — the growth came through the tape system, not the soap.
  • Freedom Distributing / Internet Services Corporation — Yager's corporate vehicles (ch7). Freedom Distributing, formed in 1980 with his family, was the financial hub of the tools business; it became Internet Services Corp. in 1989, and the tape empire reached "all 50 states and 10 foreign countries".

The regulators, the courts & the named documents

  • The FTC (Federal Trade Commission) — the regulator whose 1970s campaign frames chapter 7: it had already taken apart the proto-MLMs (Holiday Magic, Koscot) when it moved on Amway in 1975, and the video keeps the corporation's later fear of it as a planted fuse ("that point becomes important later on").
  • The 1979 FTC decision (In re Amway Corporation) — the ruling Amway has trumpeted as its seal of legality. Applies solely to the smaller pyramid; the tools business was neither declared legal nor illegal by it. Record detail (lesson 03's check): Docket 9023; complaint 25 March 1975, Commission final order 8 May 1979, reported as In re Amway Corp., 93 F.T.C. 618 (1979). It held Amway was not an illegal pyramid, but it also found two violations — resale-price maintenance and misleading earnings claims — so it was a limited order, not an acquittal. The video's "safe harbor conditions" are the order's operating rules (70% rule, a retail-customer qualification, buy-back of resalable inventory, no headhunting fee, no purchase required to join); modern commentary stresses no single rule creates a statutory safe harbor.
  • The internal study (late 1982) — commissioned by Amway after the crosslining affair, "a comprehensive internal study of the entire tools business"; the chapter says it is how the next document comes to exist. Named in the lessons; not opened there.
  • The Postma memo ("Analysis of the Britt/Yager System", 10 Jan 1983) — opened in chapter 8. Amway lawyer Ed Postma — the video's title for him head of business conduct (the memo's letterhead gives no title; later descriptions call him business conduct manager) — to Patric Sullivan [sic in the memo], the video's title for him director of sales for the Northwest region (the memo header gives the name only; the video's captions say "Patrick" while the memo spells it Patric — canonical here). Key holdings: the Yager diamonds want the corporation to "manufacture products and get out of the way" so they can run a motivation business "with little or no competition"; the diamonds "consider their personal business to be the motivation tools business" and "this is where the big money is made"; rally economics (Yager "rakes in 25 or $50,000 for a weekend or $250,000 for free enterprise days"); vendors at rallies rent jewellery, flashy clothes and cars so distributors can appear successful — "a flat-out admission that the higher pins don't make the kind of money they claim"; and the smoking gun, direct quote: "The tool business is illegal. Although the Amway business is legal, the tools business is not. It is a pyramid." Record note: the memo's text is public via the critic archive (Postma memo scan, CMU archive) — hosted by a critic (David Touretzky's CMU collection), the scan itself is the primary. Discrepancy kept visible, not harmonised: the archived Amquix "Dateline" analysis page dates the Postma analysis 19 Jan 1983; the memo header reads 10 Jan 1983 — both stay stated.
  • "The Challenge of the '80s" — internal Amway Policy Committee memo, August 1982, slightly earlier than Postma; warned that the illegality of the tools business threatened the very existence of the Amway Corporation (i.e. a renewed FTC assault). Named, not opened: existence reported (excerpts in litigation exhibits and press); full text not public.
  • The "Compliance with the Code of Ethics and Rules of Conduct" memo — correction: an internal Amway Sales Division memo, author and date unknown, probably 1982 — not a federal-court warning, though the video calls it a warning "issued by a federal district court in Ohio". Entered as a plaintiff's exhibit in Cairns v. Amway, Cincinnati, 1984. Quoted content: "widespread illegalities inherent in Amway designed systems of tapes, books, and rallies", conceived in the late 1960s–early 1970s as genuine support programs, then developed by "entrepreneurial higher pins" for "substantial separate additional income under the Amway umbrella". Original unlocated; the video quotes a source quoting it.
  • Directly Speaking (two cassettes, January 1983) — correction: spoken by Rich DeVos alone per the court-exhibit transcripts — the video says "DeVos and Van Andel". Messages to the diamonds generally and the Yager organisation particularly: a warning that the tools business could "call down the thunder" (an FTC renewal); criticism of "the curiosity approach" (see practice section); and the bombshell — "we are going to put BV on tapes": Amway-approved tapes sold at $2.50, carrying BV but no PV, with "BV on tapes can never exceed twenty percent of your total Business Volume". The kingpins read it as a declaration of war: the corporation entering the tools business to squeeze them out. Record note: the archived Amquix transcript page (Amquix archive) carries the full notarised transcripts — "Directly Speaking, January 1983, Spoken by Rich DeVos, Amway Cassette Series VA-2160", certificates (Hamilton County, Ohio, notary Kathleen M. McClellan) dated 29 March 1985 for VAL-2150 and 15 November 1984 for VA-2160; the second tape recorded 4 March 1983 ("today is my birthday. It's the 4th day of March, and I'm 57").
  • 60 Minutes, "Soap and Hope" (CBS, January 1983) — exposé on whether Amway's income promises match reality, tangentially about the tools; Mike Wallace interviews Dexter and Birdie Yager, goes "fairly easy" on them per the video, and leaves the tools-vs-Amway income question open. Record note: sources cite 9 January 1983 for the broadcast. Together with the Leonard Hall debacle, the video infers it "evidently spooked" the suits into action.
  • The "Miami Showdown" tape (5 April 1983) — opened in chapter 9. The video's description: a raw, unedited recording of the outdoor-terrace meeting at the Fontainebleau Hotel, Miami Beach, between Amway representatives (Ron Lindebom — not Lindebaum; captions garble — and Bill (William) Abraham, outside counsel for distributors, brother of Rick J. Abraham, Abraham Law Offices, Columbus OH) and tools kingpins (Jerry Herrera, Ron Hale, Doug Wead, Dexter and Birdie Yager, others — the video reports that he has not found a roster of attendees). Acrimonious at the start, cordial ("Kumbaya") at the close (the video's description; no independent record of the meeting exists). Holdings: admissions the corporation knew most money was made in tools; the leap from ~$200m to ~$1.5–2bn attributed on-tape to "seminars and rallies, the tapes, and so on, voluntarily purchased" (the speaker corrects himself mid-sentence); the Wead–Abraham "put more money in the plan" exchange (impossible without crossing the 1979 decision's red lines); Lindebom's lawyer-drafted directive — tools must stay "reasonably related to the sale of the products to the ultimate consumer"; Abraham's "chapter 13 or bankruptcy" passage and the liberals-as-enemy political statement; Yager's quiet "rejected lovers" plea for a "love relationship and mutual respect". A second, scripted session the same evening with the lower-level emeralds survives only incomplete, with fewer revelations — the second meeting the same evening is confirmed, its files named in the audio index (ec_1983_1.zip, ec_1983_2.zip). The chapter's verdict: "the corporation surrendered" — the next chapter's subject. Record note (resolved): the tape was first published on amquix.info, June 2003 (operator Scott Larsen); the archived page lists dc_1983_*.zip, ec_1983_*.zip, cut clips. The source page calls the recording location a rooftop where the video says "terrace" — wording difference noted. No archive.org item exists; the audio survives as Wayback captures. The best-documented early anti-Amway archive otherwise is the David Touretzky collection at CMU, which hosts the Postma memo and Directly Speaking transcripts but no Miami recording. The fullest published text of the tape's exchanges is Munger's own Substack serialisation, part 3 (November 2022). The name "Miami Showdown" is the video-maker's own coinage (Munger says he coined it, May 2025 post) — read it as this course's label for the meeting, not an insider name.
  • The Fontainebleau Hotel, Miami Beach — site of both the founding pitch (~1970/71) and the 1983 showdown. Corrections: opened 20 December 1954 (the video says 1955 — official hotel history; 1955 is a common secondary error); the Sinatra/Elvis special taped 26 March 1960 and aired 12 May 1960; the Bond film was Goldfinger (1964).
  • The "reasonably related… to the ultimate consumer" gloss — the retail-to-consumer test Amway's people invoke (Lindebom on the Miami tape, in the video's account: tools must not grow past being "reasonably related to the sale of the products to the ultimate consumer"). Record note: not found verbatim in 93 F.T.C. 618; the phrase family matches Robinson-Patman functional-discount boilerplate ("reasonably related to the expenses assumed by the buyer") — Amway's own compliance language, not a quotation from the 1979 decision. Correction: the video's ch9 "Holiday Interplanetary" is a slip for Koscot Interplanetary (with Holiday Magic) — the enterprise the suits fear "going the way of".
  • The Ten Points (January 1983 pledges) — opened in chapter 10. The pledges from the Directly Speaking tape (Diamond Bulletin, February 1983) — the "10 points" DeVos is asked on the stand whether he "deviated from". His answer: "we never pursued it to its ultimate goal of really nailing anything down." A document, not a node.
  • Setzer v. Amway (filed 17 July 1986; tried April 1988) — opened in chapter 10. Rick Setzer and Sue Lynn Setzer v. Amway Corp., Richard M. DeVos and Jay Van Andel, Greenville, SC (6:86-1898-3); plaintiff Rick Setzer a Triple Diamond suing over interference with his tools business; judgment $160,938.72. Holds DeVos's sworn surrender Q&A ("those abuses continued to this day"; "upward of $300 million in volume"). Read the quotes as a court transcript excerpt on a critic-hosted page. The video says the case is unclear from its sources.
  • The 1986 consent decree — $100,000 over 1983 newspaper ads violating the earnings-disclosure order (monthly income $67; Ruby Bonus $288 average). The only pre-1990 federal follow-up found.
  • WWG + LTD — World Wide Group and Leadership Team Development, co-defendants with Amway in the September 2026 filing.
  • The September 2026 FTC + Washington action — opened in chapter 12. Filed 17 September 2026 against Amway, WWG and LTD; proposed $225m settlement (W.D. Wash. 2:26-cv-03474); the first FTC action at the Approved Provider/BSM training system. Labelled filed/proposed: allegations, no final judgment; postdates the video, which said the tools business was "officially untested".
  • Hart v. Amway (filed 8 Apr 1997) — Brig and Lita Hart v. Amway, M.D. Fla., Case 97-349-CIV-J-20B. Para 57 pleads "on information and belief" that over 70% of Yager's Amway-related income came from tools — "$40,000,000.00 per year in gross income". Pleaded, not found: read the figure as an allegation, not a finding.

Roles, ranks & structure

  • Distributor / IBO ("independent business owner") — anyone who joins. Amway's own term is IBO; the video's point: the jargon turns a buyer into a "business owner".
  • Line of sponsorship — the chain of recruiters a distributor joins; upline = the recruiter above you; downline = those you recruit and their recruits. Upline–downline chains "are very similar to family genealogies, another reason why Amway organizations resemble the structure of the mafia" (ch4).
  • MLM (multi-level marketing) — income primarily from recruiting further sellers rather than retail customers; Amway as archetype.
  • Going direct / Direct Distributor — ordering enough product to buy directly from Amway instead of through your upline's house; in the 1982 Wisconsin record "Direct Distributors" are the top tier (~200 of ~20,000 distributorships) whose tax returns produced the $918 average net loss (checked in lesson 01).
  • Pin levels — the jewel hierarchy: pearl, ruby, emerald, and the brass ring, diamond (higher levels were later invented as kingpins' businesses grew). "Going diamond" is the movement's euphemism for financial success — "as if it's a foregone conclusion".
  • Diamonds — the Amway stars fêted at rallies alongside the tool kingpins.
  • Point transfers — ch7's mechanism for keeping the tools profits closed: the kingpins moved volume ("points") between themselves, so they could create or destroy a diamond — and the lines of business attached — at will. The chapter's name for the result is a walled garden: the riches of the tools business reach only a chosen few, decided by that few.
  • BV (business volume) & PV (point value) — the two figures Amway bonuses are calculated on: BV the dollar figure, PV the unit figure. Ch9's flashpoint: Wead's on-tape "BV on tapes" reference voices the kingpins' fear that Amway-approved tapes carrying BV would let the corporation into the motivation business and squeeze them out (see Directly Speaking: tapes at $2.50, no PV, BV capped at twenty percent of total Business Volume).
  • Emerald — pin level above platinum/direct, below diamond. The Miami afternoon's second, scripted ballroom session was for these lower-level tools participants; its tape is incomplete, with fewer revelations.
  • Kingpins — the men at the top of the tools business; named so far: Bill Britt, Dexter Yager, Doug Wead, and Rex Renfrow (ch3). Britt, Yager and Wead are the ones with speaking parts. Chapter 9 adds Jerry Herrera and Ron Hale to the named roster (see people section).

The practice & vocabulary

  • "Showing the plan" / "drawing circles" — the classic recruitment meeting: butcher paper, markers, circles standing for recruits and their recruits; Amway often named only at the end.
  • Rallies & seminars — the live arm of the tools: pageantry and "populist hysteria", stars fêted "like conquering Roman emperors or rock stars"; tape tables at the entrances; tapes often recorded straight off the stage — "a snake eating its own tail". The 1980 Charlotte rally ended with distributors in the streets at 3 a.m. chanting "Five and six nights a week" — the nights they intend to spend drawing circles.
  • Free Enterprise Days — the immense rallies held at a sports arena in Charlotte (ch7), the ones where Yager arrived by coach and the crowd chanted "five and six nights a week"; chapter 7 files them under the tools' theatre of wealth. (On the record, the rallies drew 80,000-plus; see lesson 03.)
  • Crosslining — ch7's "ultimate sin in the Amway universe": poaching distributors across separate lines of sponsorship, "like gangsters from one leg of the family going into business with another separate branch… an act of disloyalty" in the mafia analogy. Beyond loyalty, it breaks the architecture — separate lines are insulated from each other so one collapse doesn't take the rest down. Amway terminated Leonard Hall's distributorship over it.
  • "No soap millionaires, but tools cult millionaires" — ch7's summary of where the money really was. The recruiting lore "Amway produced more millionaires than any other business" is documented as a talking point and false as fact (video: "patently and quantifiably nonsense"; lesson 03's check confirms the line and the falseness).
  • "The curiosity approach" — bait-and-switch prospecting that conceals Amway's name: luring people into meetings without telling them they are signing up for Amway. Criticised by DeVos himself on the Directly Speaking tapes: "You must tell 'em it's a business opportunity meeting. If they ask you if it's Amway, you say 'yes.'"
  • Chapter 13 — the US Bankruptcy Code's wage-earner reorganisation chapter. On the Miami tape, Abraham tells the kingpins "you can't give up your income because if you did it means chapter 13 or bankruptcy" — i.e. giving up tools income means ruin.
  • The Wisconsin study (as told in chapter 2) — "in 1980, the Wisconsin Attorney General crunched the numbers of the top 1% of Amway distributors in that state"; nearly all lost money net of operating costs; average loss put at $900 (checked in lesson 01: tax years 1979–80, case filed July 1982, the figure is $918).

The people (as of chapter 12)

  • Dexter Yager — the tape business's distribution half and, by ch7, "the biggest of the kingpins… the largest volume of Amway business of anybody" (confirmed on the record: his network of 1 million distributors was called Amway's largest). Born 6 December 1939 in Fulton, New York; raised in Rome (the video says Rome — conflating birthplace with hometown; lesson 02's check); poor family; his own hagiography stresses a born-salesman childhood that the video doubts; a car salesman and "beer salesman with an outfit called the West End Brewery"; early heavy drinker. Joins Amway 1964; moves to Charlotte, NC in 1969 with Birdie and the children. Sponsors Renard (1966); declines the $50,000 tape-plant pitch; distributes Britt's tapes. Ch7's wealth portrait: a mansion outside Charlotte treated as a pilgrimage site, Rolls-Royces, yachts, a coach for rallies, the Free Enterprise Days rallies — and, per the video, "$30 million a year" by 1990 (lesson 03's check could not trace that figure; the record's numbers are smaller — and the one pleaded number is larger: the Hart v. Amway complaint, para 57, pleads "on information and belief" that over 70% of Yager's Amway-related income was tools money, "$40,000,000.00 per year in gross income" — an allegation, not a finding). (Captions sometimes render the name "Yeager"; canonical here: Yager.)
  • Birdie Yager — Dexter's wife; four children at the Charlotte move, eventually seven.
  • Tony Renard — sponsored into Amway by Yager, Fall 1966; buys a second-hand tape recorder from him; begins duplicating motivational tapes; by the late '60s tapes are moving through Yager's downline — "at first, they're not a profit center". Resolved: Wead's 2009 blog post (the true source of the founding quote, not The Magic Makers) identifies him — a piano player in Schenectady who bought a reel-to-reel from Dexter for $46.75; wife Sue. The 1966 sponsorship half stays the video's account.
  • Dominic Conigliaro — Amway distributor sponsored by Yager; in 1970 he sponsors Bill Britt.
  • Bill Britt — the tape business's manufacturing half. "Virtually a black box" biography: U.S. Army service in the Korean War; municipal official in central North Carolina (the video says "Charlotte area"). Joins Amway 1970 (sponsor: Conigliaro). At the Fontainebleau meeting he answered the nameless man's pitch with "I'll do it." Chapter 7 adds scale: his and Yager's downlines "accounted for the lion's share of the Amway Corporation's profits".
  • Fred Hanson — "one of the original Amway distributors", reportedly sponsored by Rich DeVos in the '50s (the record says the Basses — lesson 02); possibly the man who lent Yager the Nightingale record. The video keeps this uncertain. Spelling: the video says Hansen; Wead's 2009 blog spells it Hanson — canonical here: Hanson.
  • ? The nameless young man — the person Wead's account says pitched the tape-plant investment at the Fontainebleau Hotel coffee shop, "for a $50,000 investment". Identity unknown; Wead himself is considered and set aside (too young, hadn't met Yager yet by most accounts).
  • Doug Wead — tools kingpin, source of the founding account, and by ch6 the story's connective tissue. Born Indiana 1946; lifelong Pentecostal and Assemblies of God preacher; motivational speaker from the mid-1970s; employed by Yager as assistant, press agent and ghostwriter, co-authoring books pushed as tools (probably the writer of Yager's 1978 Don't Let Anybody Steal Your Dream); coined "compassionate capitalism", the phrase Rich DeVos used as a 1994 book title and George W. Bush as a 2000 campaign theme; wrote a Reagan biography dedicated to Yager; became an Amway distributor himself around 1981; later White House staff under the first President Bush (1989), cashiered in 1990; ran for Congress in Arizona in 1992, losing after Barry Goldwater endorsed his opponent; advised the second President Bush, secretly taped their conversations, and released the tapes in 2005; later advised the Pauls. Now deceased. The video's verdict on him as a source: probably right on facts, unreliable on spin — "famously a chameleon".
  • Betty Jo Renfrow — wife of tools kingpin Rex Renfrow; told Wead at a rally: "Don't you ever talk about the tape of the week to our group… We have the tape of the day, or the tape of the hour." (Captions render the name "Betty Joe Renfro"; the record's spelling is Betty Jo Renfrow — lesson 02.)
  • Gerald R. Ford — the 38th president, and ch7's "secret weapon": the long-time Republican congressman from Grand Rapids, Michigan, the seat next door to Amway's Ada. Became vice president in 1973 after Spiro Agnew's resignation and president in August 1974 after Watergate. The video asserts that DeVos and Van Andel "visited him privately in the White House" in 1975 — the visit's content is unrecorded, and the video itself says any deal "we may never know". Record (lesson 03): the Ford–Amway relationship is documented (Ford a fixture at Amway conventions; DeVos recorded in the Oval Office in Politico's obituary; Ford cutting ribbons at early Amway launches), and the Ford papers hold Amway-related material (1965 convention speech; Aug 1975 White House press file) — but no record of the private 1975 meeting's conversation was found; the Daily Diaries are the unsearched-by-us place a visit would appear.
  • Phil Kerns — former Amway distributor and author of Fake It 'Til You Make It, "the first book-length exposé of Amway and the tools cult" (Victory Press, 1982; the "first" is a critic's assessment). Ch7's irony: Kerns had earlier co-authored a book with Doug Wead — Peoples Temple, Peoples Tomb (1979), on the Jonestown cult that killed over 900 people in Guyana in November 1978.
  • Leonard Hall — the West Coast distributor who "goes rogue" in late 1982: poached distributors along the California coast, hooking them on his own tools line — crosslining — and had his distributorship terminated. The video says it "knows very little" about him and draws the account "mostly from the Miami Showdown tape". One independent trace: the archived Amquix attorneys page records "a crown distributorship, Leonard Hall in California was selling his system to the distributors who had no system at the time… in the early 1980's" — consistent with the video's account; no press or court trace beyond a distributor wiki (lesson 03). Treat the person and the affair as the video's account with one trace.
  • William Penn Patrick — founder of Holiday Magic Inc. (1964), a cosmetics MLM that became one of the early infamous pyramid schemes; founder of Leadership Dynamics (1967), a pioneer of large group awareness training; holder of a stake in Mind Dynamics; member of the John Birch Society; died 1973, after which his companies closed. Ch5's emblem of the MLM–ultra-conservatism link.
  • Alexander Everett — Patrick's mentor; founder of Mind Dynamics (per lesson 02's check, 1968; Patrick later held a stake), the "cult" group whose techniques the video links to this whole motivational family.
  • Glenn W. Turner — an employee of Patrick's Holiday Magic before founding Koscot Interplanetary Inc. and, in 1969, Dare to Be Great!, selling graduated self-motivation courses on cassette — four "Adventure" levels totalling $8,000 (over $61,000 today), in the same message Yager would push a few years later. Amassed a $150m fortune; Koscot was driven out of business by the FTC in 1975; indicted with F. Lee Bailey in 1973 (hung jury); went to prison in 1987 over a later pyramid scheme; died 2020.
  • Werner Erhard — involved in Mind Dynamics; founder of EST (Erhard Seminars Training), the era's other famous human-potential organisation, its OT-style graduated levels echoing Scientology's (ch5).
  • F. Lee Bailey — celebrity defence lawyer indicted in 1973 on conspiracy charges tied to Koscot and Turner; the case ended in a hung jury (ch5).
  • Ronald Reagan — brought by Yager to speak at a massive Amway rally in Charlotte in the late summer of 1980, while a candidate; the subject of Wead's dedicated-to-Yager biography (ch6).
  • The Bush family (the two President Bushes) — Wead's long-running political connection: close to the first President Bush in the 1980s, later White House aide to him, then adviser to the second President Bush (whose secretly taped conversations Wead released in 2005) (ch6).
  • Ed Postma — Amway lawyer, the video's title for him head of the business-conduct division (the memo's letterhead gives no title; later descriptions call him business conduct manager); author of the 10 January 1983 memo "Analysis of the Britt/Yager System" (ch8).
  • Patric Sullivan — the video's title for him Amway director of sales, Northwest region (the memo header gives the name only); recipient of the Postma memo. (The video's captions say "Patrick"; the memo spells it "Patric" — canonical here.)
  • Ron Lindebom — Amway director of sales, western region; the corporation's spokesman at the Miami Showdown alongside Bill (William) Abraham. The lineage & the parallels (ch5's widening)

  • The Strangest Secret (1956) — Earl Nightingale's spoken-word record; "the earliest motivational tape" and the model for the whole duplication practice; reaches Yager mid-'60s (possibly via Hanson).
  • Napoleon Hill — author of Think and Grow Rich (1937), later at the top of the classic Amway reading list; Nightingale's inspiration; in the video's telling "a con man with a long string of failed businesses".
  • Dale Carnegie — How to Win Friends and Influence People; part of the same 1930s–50s self-help wave the tools recycled; the video notes it "inspired, among others, Charles Manson" (the record's version: Manson cribbed the book in prison — lesson 02).
  • Norman Vincent Peale — conservative evangelical preacher; his New York congregation included the Trump family; another node of the same wave.
  • Holiday Magic — the 1964 cosmetics MLM founded by William Penn Patrick on the Amway model; one of the early infamous pyramids; also a motivation business (Leadership Dynamics; a stake in Mind Dynamics); prosecuted by California, the SEC and the FTC; ended with Patrick's death in 1973 and the FTC's 1974 order to stop its deceptive plan (lesson 03's check).
  • Koscot Interplanetary — Glenn W. Turner's pyramid (the ASR "Koskot" is a garble; the name fuses "Kos" (cosmetics) and "cot" (communities of tomorrow), with "interplanetary" for a futuristic flavour (NYT, 13 Jan 1972)); driven out of business by the FTC's 1975 order; its motivational course business — Dare to Be Great! — is the chapter's key precedent that motivation itself, on cassette, was a profitable commodity.
  • Mind Dynamics / EST / the "human potential movement" — the 1970s sibling organisations the video gathers under one roof: Mind Dynamics (Everett; a 1972 book-length exposé), Werner Erhard's EST, and the Scientology comparison ("for a time popularly believed to be a part of this movement"). The video's point: the tools cult did not invent its shape; it inherited it.
  • The John Birch Society — the far-right anti-communist organisation Patrick belonged to; the video's first hard line between MLM motivation and ultra-conservative politics ("nearly all of the tools cult kingpins would turn out to be conservative evangelical Republicans"), with a forward thread to chapter 11.
  • The Koscot–Amway personnel link — ch5's claim, sourced to the Miami Showdown tape: several former Koscot distributors later joined Amway, "and some apparently became tools cult kingpins". Asserted on the tape, not independently documented (lesson 03).
  • The Fontainebleau meeting (~1970/71) — the founding scene of the tools business, as Wead's account tells it: Nameless pitches; Yager declines; Britt accepts. Resolved: the quote's true source is Wead's 2009 blog post, not The Magic Makers (1982; controlled-lending, IA id magicmakershowto00yage) — read the scene as one insider's account, flagged (lesson 02's fact-check).
  • FGBMFI + prosperity gospel (ch11) — the Full Gospel Business Men's Fellowship International, the Pentecostal businessmen's network (incorporated January 1952); the video's bridge between the tools cult and televangelism, crediting it with "essentially" building prosperity-gospel ideology (the video's gloss on the scholarship). Yager's membership is thesis-supported with no roll located; fellow-travellers: Oral Roberts (founding figure), John Osteen (early tie; Joel by lineage only), Jim Bakker.
  • Scholarship touching the tools business — for the video's "first attempted by a historian" claim (kept with its "to my knowledge" qualifier): Harris (Harvard 1992) + Mondom (Syracuse 2019, surface.syr.edu/etd/1090) are the unidentified dissertations, now identified; plus Burch 2015 and Biggart 1989. No book-length scholarly history of the tools/BSM business surfaced (lesson 07).
