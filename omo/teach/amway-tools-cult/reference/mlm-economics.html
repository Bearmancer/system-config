Reference — the money mechanics of Amway and the tools business

Where the money comes from — and why it looks like profit

Companion to the course, built chapter-7-first · as of chapter 7 · course home: index · glossary · cast map

The question this page answers is the one chapter 7 leaves you holding: the tools business grew on the same distributors Amway sold product to, no outside customer had to be found for any of it, and yet the era is full of men getting visibly, spectacularly rich. Where does "gain" come from when no new money enters the circle? The short answer this page assembles from the cases below: most of the money never enters — it circulates. Bonuses and rebates are paid out of the participants' own purchases; the company books revenue when it ships to distributors, not when an outsider buys; the tools' margin sits in different owners' pockets entirely; and the financing that grows the machine is the participants' own cash, collected before any bonus is paid. Chapter 7 is where all four of those facts are on the table at once. The modern enforcement cases cited below are external evidence about how MLM money works, named only where the economics is the point.

1. Chapter 7 first: the money that never touches Amway's books

Chapter 7's structural claim is the key to every later question about the economics: the tools are a different business with different owners. The chapter says it plainly — the real nature of Yager's business was "selling tapes, books, and rally tickets. The Amway Corporation didn't get a piece of that." So the first accounting fact is a corporate fact: the MLM's published financials, whatever they show, are not a picture of the tools business at all. Its revenue never lands in Amway's books; its margin never shows up as Amway's profit.

The second fact is dependence. The chapter says Yager, Britt "and their associated downlines accounted for the lion's share of the Amway Corporation's profits", and that Amway's own revenue "could exist so only so long as the tools kingpins, the diamonds, kept their downlines hooked on motivational tapes." Read that as a cash-flow statement: the corporation's income is downstream of the same people buying the tools. The tools owners are not parasites on Amway from Amway's point of view — they are the pump that keeps the downlines buying product. That is why the corporation could not afford to look hard at them, and why, on the chapter's account, it did not until events forced it.

Third: the chapter's own money summary — "there were no soap millionaires, but there were tools cult millionaires." If the actual money in the system is made in the tools, then the standard picture ("you get rich selling Amway products") is false inside the system's own terms. The men who look rich were being paid by the distributors, not by outside customers.

2. The circle: where money enters, and where it only moves

Four parties, one loop. The table below is the whole mechanism on one screen; everything after this section is detail.

Who Money in Money out / back
Distributor (participant) Their own wallet: product purchases, standing orders, tools, rally tickets, later subscriptions and ads. Bonuses and rebates on purchases (their own and their downline's) — a share of the money that came from people in the same circle.
Amway Corporation Wholesale value of goods shipped to distributors; small entry kit. Bonus/rebate payments (an operating expense), refunds on buy-backs — never a share of the tools' revenue.
Tools owners (kingpins) Tapes, books, rallies — historically; voicemail, digital media, subscriptions and apps later. Almost nothing back into the circle: the margin leaves the loop and stays with the owners.
Outside retail customer The only money that enters from outside: paying a distributor for products. No bonus, no rebate, no tools purchase.

In one line: the loop is participant → company → bonus → participant, plus participant → tools owners → nothing returns. That is what "circular financing" means here: the same dollars are counted at more than one place — as revenue at the company, as a bonus to the participant, and as tools income to the kingpins — while only the outside-customer arrow brings anything new in. The tools arrow is the one that makes men rich, because it is the one with no return path.

3. The rebate: why a "bonus" is not profit

Amway's plan is a volume rebate, published as points. In Amway's own Business Reference Guide, PV (point value) is a unit amount assigned to each product and BV (business volume) a dollar figure assigned to each product, with a monthly bonus ladder running from 3% of BV at 100–299 PV to 25% at 7,500+ PV (the BV:PV ratio is currently published at 3.43:1). Then come the layers above the ladder: differential bonus — you are paid the difference between your percentage and your downline's, e.g. "12% − 6% = 6%" — and, at the top, monthly Leadership/Depth bonuses plus annual Emerald and Diamond payouts.

Two consequences follow, and they are the reason a "25% bonus" and "profit" are not the same word:

  • It is a discount, priced by volume. The percentage moves with purchases, is paid on purchases, and is funded by purchases. Economically it functions as a volume rebate on your own buying — an explainer framing, not a term of art in any source (see the checks below).
  • Buying your own product is how the ladder is climbed. Nothing on a PV ladder requires an outside customer to exist. That is exactly the pressure the Amway safeguards target: the 70% rule (70% of an IBO's personal BV each month must come from products sold at a commercially reasonable price), the customer rule (sales to at least 10 different customers, or 50 PV of sales), the buy-back rule (Amway repurchases unused, currently marketable products), and the small entry kit — the 1979 decision records "A new Amway distributor is not required to buy inventory. The distributor need only buy a $15.60 'Sales Kit'."

Read those rules as a list of what the business looks like without them: inventory loading, self-consumption, and a bonus paid on money the participant never earned from anyone else. The rules exist to keep some of the circle's money coming from outside.

4. Whose books show the gain?

This is where "net gain" gets slippery, because two different sets of books are being compared, often without saying so.

  • The company's books look strong precisely because revenue is recognised when goods transfer to the distributor. Under the revenue standard (ASU 2014-09/ASC 606), revenue is recognised when a customer obtains control of the goods — and in an MLM, the distributor is the customer. Outsiders are not required for the top line to grow. Bonuses and rebates sit below the top line as an expense, and expected returns/buy-backs are carried as a refund liability (rights of return are treated as variable consideration). So a healthy-looking statement of profit is entirely compatible with its customers losing money.
  • The participant's books are the other half. The record is blunt where it has been tested: in the FTC's Success By Health case, "less than 2 percent of participating consumers received more money from the defendants than they paid to them, and … averaged less than $250 per month … After accounting for the costs of the program, products, and events, enrollees lost millions." The FTC's AdvoCare complaint describes a pay plan that "incentivized distributors to purchase large quantities of AdvoCare products — even when they couldn't sell the products" (a $150m settlement). The AARP Foundation's 2018 survey of MLM participants found 47% lost money, 27% broke even, and 25% made a profit. And the chapter-2 thread of this course already carries the dated Wisconsin finding: the top tier of distributors, net of operating costs, averaged a loss.
  • The famous exception — the men the tools business made rich — is not a counter-example to the circle; it is the circle's exit valve. They were paid by participants, from a business with no outside demand requirement, and their money left the loop.

So the honest answer to "why is there a net gain?" is: for whom? At the company level, gain is manufactured by selling to the distribution network. At the participant level, the measured reality is mostly loss. At the top of the tools business, gain is real and comes out of the participants' side of the circle.

5. Corporate financing: the float

Where does the growth capital come from if not outside investors? Largely from the participants, and before any bonus is owed:

  • Cash before obligations. Money arrives at wholesale when product ships or when a standing order bills; bonuses are computed later, on volume for a period already paid for. The company holds the cash in between. A listed MLM's own filings show the mechanism — Herbalife's cash-flow statement carries "Advance sales deposits" and its balance sheet a "Royalty overrides" payable, on top of a shareholders' deficit. "Float" is the analytic name for this; it is not an accounting term.
  • Standing orders as subscriptions. A distributor agreeing to buy a tape every week "regardless of what it is, until cancelled — which it would almost never be" (chapter 3) is recurring, predictable, prepaid revenue with no external demand behind it. This page's judgment: that is the single most important financial invention in this story.
  • Inventory loading transfers risk downward. Every unit shipped to a distributor is revenue to the company and inventory risk to the participant — unless they can sell it, and unless a buy-back obligation returns their cash. The safeguards in §3 exist because this is otherwise an interest-free loan from the bottom of the pyramid to the top — this page's framing, not a sourced term.

This is also the cleanest line between a legal MLM and a pyramid scheme. The peer-reviewed comparison: legal MLM firms "rely mostly on sales to consumers outside the MLM", while pyramid schemes "rely mostly on purchases within the pyramid structure" (Reingewertz, PLOS ONE, 2021). The SEC's description of a Ponzi scheme — paying "existing investors with funds collected from new investors … a constant flow of new money to survive" — describes what happens when the outside arrow stops. The chapter-7 story is the story of a system whose outside arrow was never the main one.

6. Why "no new money" caps the whole thing

Inside the circle the flows are transfers, not income. Add up everyone's net position and you get: total participant purchases, minus product cost, minus company overhead and margin, minus tools margin — negative on the cases measured (see the caveat below), before counting anyone's time. The only variable that can postpone that arithmetic is new participants' money. That is why these structures are described by their regulators in terms of needing a constant inflow, and why the academic finding in the same study is that the pyramid form is mathematically doomed at the point where new recruitment slows (one case study cited there: maximum penetration of about 1% of a state's population, with 94% of participants making no profit).

Caveat that matters: no regulator writes the sentence "money circulates; nothing new is created". The synthesis above is assembled from the SEC's Ponzi definition, the peer-reviewed legal-MLM/pyramid distinction, and enforcement findings — not quoted from one authority.

7. The digital footprint, part one: the tools go digital

Nothing about the mechanics changes when the medium changes — which is exactly why the tools business moved into every new medium it could:

  • Tapes (the founding medium). Manufacturing was a real business — Britt's plant, Yager's distribution, the sweetheart wholesale deal. Chapter 7 shows the corporate name tracking the medium: Yager's Freedom Distributing (1980) became Internet Services Corporation (1989), and the empire reached "all 50 states and 10 foreign countries". (Charlotte Observer series; Fifth Circuit, P&G v. Amway.)
  • Voicemail. Amway itself ran a distributor voicemail system, Amvox — court and press records describe "Amway's electronic voice mail system, Amvox" as the channel for broadcasting to distributors, and the AMVOX mark was Amway's own. A phone message replaces a cassette: no manufacturing, no shipping, no inventory — but the same standing-order logic.
  • Digital BSM. In Amway's current rules, business support materials cover "audio, video, live streaming and digital media … blogs, podcasts and mobile applications", sold only through its accredited-provider scheme. The content stayed motivational; the cost structure became software: near-zero marginal cost per copy, recurring subscription billing, and no returns to finance. "Near-zero marginal cost" is this page's description, not a sourced quote.

Why this matters to the accounting question: the tools were already the profit centre with no return path; digitising them removes the manufacturing and inventory costs and converts one-off purchases into subscriptions. The circle gets tighter, the margin gets cleaner, and nothing about the underlying flow changes — participants still fund it, and the money still leaves the loop.

8. The digital footprint, part two: the recruiting layer

The second digital shift is on the distributor's side: the "footprint" — the ads, funnels, courses, tools and training a distributor pays for in order to look successful and recruit. Regulators treat this as part of the cost structure, not an accident of it. The FTC's own business guidance says "Most MLM participants incur numerous expenses, such as costs for product purchases, travel for conferences, tools or services, and training" — and adds that buy-back provisions "do not protect against harm from" travel, tools, services and training, because those are cash out with nothing to return. Its cases show the pressure: Success By Health affiliates were pushed to "max out their credit cards, borrow from family … to buy inventory and attend costly 'training' programs", and recent actions restate the finding that most people make little or no money.

Put that next to §5 and the digital recruiting layer is simply the float in reverse: cash out of the participant's pocket, into tools and training owned by people inside the circle, with no external sale required at any point. A distributor's professional-looking online presence is not evidence the business pays; it is often itself one of the products being sold to them.

9. What the record shows — checking the economics claims

Fact-check — verified against FTC and SEC materials, Amway plan documents, accounting standards, peer-reviewed work and surveys
  • "The circle is funded by participants" — confirmed case-by-case; no industry-wide figure exists. In FTC v. Vemma, "the sole product seller was the parent company and the only significant product buyers were the distributors" (a critic site summarising the court record — advocacy, labelled). FTC v. AdvoCare (2019, $150m) found a plan that pushed distributors to buy "large quantities … even when they couldn't sell the products". Correction: no authority publishes an industry-wide internal-vs-retail percentage — so any specific split (including the video's 2%/98%) stays unsourced. (Truth in Advertising, on Vemma; FTC, AdvoCare.)
  • "Most participants lose" — confirmed on the record. Success By Health: "less than 2 percent of participating consumers received more money from the defendants than they paid to them, and … averaged less than $250 per month … After accounting for the costs of the program, products, and events, enrollees lost millions." AARP Foundation's 2018 survey (n=601): 47% lost money, 27% broke even, 25% made a profit. A case study cited in peer review found 94% of participants made no profit at 1% population penetration. (FTC, Success By Health; AARP Foundation, 2018; Reingewertz, PLOS ONE 2021.)
  • PV/BV, the ladder and the differential — confirmed from Amway's own plan document. "Point Value, or PV, is a unit amount assigned to each product … Business Volume, or BV, is a dollar figure assigned by Amway to each product"; schedule from "100–299: 3% of BV" to "7,500 or more: 25% of BV"; differentials stated as "12% − 6% = 6%"; monthly Leadership/Depth bonuses plus annual Emerald/Diamond; BV:PV currently "3.43 to 1". This is the current published plan; the 1979-era plan is described in the FTC decision. (Amway Business Reference Guide.)
  • The safeguards — confirmed, with a wording correction. Current Amway rules carry the lineage: "Seventy Percent Rule: … average of 70% of the IBO's personal Business Volume (BV) per month must come from products sold at a commercially reasonable price"; Customer Volume — "make not less than one sale to each of 10 different Customers; or (b) have at least 50 PV of sales" (the 50-PV alternative is today's wording, not the 1979 text); Buy-Back Rule — "Amway shall repurchase … unused, currently marketable Amway products". The 1979 decision itself records the low entry cost: "A new Amway distributor is not required to buy inventory. The distributor need only buy a $15.60 'Sales Kit'." (Amway Rules of Conduct; FTC, 93 F.T.C. 618.)
  • The float — mechanism confirmed; the label is the explainer's. A listed MLM's filings show the pattern (advance sales deposits, royalty-overrides payable, negative equity), and the standing-order economics are chapter 3's. But "float" is analytic framing: no accounting standard or regulator uses it for MLMs. (Herbalife annual report.)
  • Revenue recognition and returns — confirmed as general accounting. Revenue is recognised when the customer obtains control (ASU 2014-09/ASC 606); rights of return are variable consideration requiring a refund liability for goods expected to come back. Applied here: the distributor is the customer, and buy-backs are the reverse flow. (FASB ASU 2014-09.)
  • The tools are separately owned — confirmed by a federal court. The Eighth Circuit: "The 'tools business' arose to meet the demand … many of the companies in the tools business are owned by individuals in the products business." That is chapter 7's corporate fact in a court's words. (8th Cir. opinion.)
  • The "no new money" synthesis — assembled, not quoted. The nearest authoritative statements: the SEC's Ponzi definition ("pays existing investors with funds collected from new investors … a constant flow of new money to survive") and the peer-reviewed distinction that legal MLMs "rely mostly on sales to consumers outside the MLM" while pyramid schemes "rely mostly on purchases within the pyramid structure". No single source says the sentence this page synthesises from them. (SEC; Reingewertz, above.)
  • The rebate framing — flagged, not sourced. Describing the distributor percentage as a volume rebate / price-discrimination mechanism rather than profits is this page's framing; no located authority states it in those terms. The plan mechanics behind the framing are sourced in §3.
  • The digital layer — confirmed for the artefacts, framed for the economics. Amvox was Amway's distributor voicemail ("Amway's electronic voice mail system, Amvox"; the AMVOX mark was Amway's); today's BSM policy covers "audio, video, live streaming and digital media … blogs, podcasts and mobile applications" through accredited providers. "Near-zero marginal cost" is the page's description of digital delivery, not a sourced quote. (Deseret News; UPI; Amway rules.)
  • The recruiting cost layer — confirmed by the regulator. FTC guidance: "Most MLM participants incur numerous expenses, such as costs for product purchases, travel for conferences, tools or services, and training", and buy-back provisions "do not protect against harm from" those costs. Its cases document the pressure to fund them on credit. (FTC business guidance on MLM; FTC consumer alert, 2020.)
  • Caveats. Advocacy arithmetic (critic-site summaries, the appended numbers in some survey PDFs) is not measured fact and is labelled as such; company-wide internal-consumption percentages are unsupported and none is stated here; Amway's plan documents are the current published version, cited for mechanics the video's era also used; where the video's own numbers remain unsourced, lesson 03's fact-check already says so.

Method — how this page was built
