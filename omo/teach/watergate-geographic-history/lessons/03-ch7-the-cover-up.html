The Cover-Up

1. What the chapter does

The chapter argues the cover-up was never a choice. The burglary belonged to a long pattern of illicit acts, and Nixon prized covert tools on principle, so confession never occurred to him. The June days after the break-in sealed that path.

3. The narrative

The chapter opens by rejecting a popular what-if. Had Nixon confessed at once, the country might have forgiven him. The narrator declines that view, for two reasons.

Reason one is that the burglary was no one-off. It belonged to a broad run of illegal and ethically dubious practices stretching back to the start of the administration, and before. Exhibit one is the Chennault affair, carried as confirmed against the university-press account of the Hughes book (see UVA Press on Chasing Shadows), with a White House memo on file at the Nixon Library titled for the affair itself (see Nixon Library memo file). Exhibit two is Nixon on tape ordering aides to break into the Brookings Institution, blow open the safe, and steal the file:

Get in and get those files.

— Nixon, as quoted in the chapter

That Brookings order is confirmed against the tape account (see Miller Center tape account).

Reason two is Nixon's cast of mind. Dirty tricks, covert operations, enemies lists, dirt-digging, disruption of hostile groups. Nixon approved of these things and insisted on them. A brief Oval Office excerpt with CIA director Helms illustrates the appetite, and the exact excerpt stays unverified here since it was not pulled against a tape log in this pass.

The chapter then walks a causal chain across the map. The Cambodia decision announced at the end of April is confirmed against the speech record (see Miller Center speech record). Thin military gain, fierce protest at home. At Kent State, guardsmen shoot protesters. Four students die, including twenty-year-old Jeffrey Miller, a toll confirmed against the university commemoration (see Kent State commemoration). Campuses revolt. A nationwide strike follows. Hundreds of thousands march on Washington.

Days later Nixon slips out to the Lincoln Memorial with his valet and talks past the campers, then writes staff a memo explaining the strange visit. His later memoirs retell it unreliably. The visit itself is confirmed against the Library account (see Nixon Library visit account). The video measurement of the Memorial-to-Watergate distance stays unverified for lack of a second witness. After Kent State, Nixon demands heavier monitoring of supposed subversives. Huston drafts the grandiose plan. Nixon reads protest as foreign-directed subversion, a lesson the chapter traces to the Caracas limo attack, which is confirmed against the HISTORY account (see HISTORY on the Caracas attack).

The Huston Plan goes to the intelligence chiefs in late June. Hoover's objection blocks it, but many of its ideas return a year later as the Plumbers. That sequence is confirmed against the declassified release (see National Security Archive release). The refrain lands hard: the Huston Plan led to Watergate, the Memorial visit led to Watergate, Kent State led to Watergate, Cambodia led to Watergate.

So Nixon could never have confessed in June. Stonewall, obstruct, deflect, divert. No other play occurred to him. The record of those days fits. How Nixon learned of the burglary is disputed between the memoirs and the Graff book, and with no primary located in this pass both readings stay unverified. That McCord was a re-election committee man and ex-CIA hand is confirmed (see Britannica on McCord). Back in Washington, Nixon tells Haldeman the caught men will need money. The exact hush-money wording stays unverified here. Morning meetings follow. One Nixon-Haldeman talk loses eighteen minutes thirty seconds to later erasure, a length confirmed against the forensic-notes report (see National Archives forensic notes). How and by whom stays the next chapter question.

Then the words that end the presidency. Nixon instructs Haldeman to have the CIA tell the FBI to halt the burglary inquiry, and keeps discussing payments to buy silence, an order confirmed against the trial-tapes record (see Nixon Library trial-tapes record):

There definitely was a cover up. Nixon instructed Haldeman and others to tell the FBI to stop investigating the burglary, and he also discussed paying money to the burglars to prevent them from talking.

— the chapter take-home point

The tapes get their own treatment next. The erasure mechanics wait one chapter.

4. The machinery

The machine here is repression as routine. Protest becomes subversion. Subversion becomes foreign control. Foreign control justifies covert tools. The Huston Plan writes the wish list. Hoover vetoes the paper but not the appetite. The Plumbers inherit the tools. The cover-up inherits the reflex.
